AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 8: Group Combat, Party, Followers, Summons, and Scale

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 7 defined individual combat consequence logic.

Chunk 8 defines what happens when combat or action involves more than one meaningful participant.

This chunk answers:

How does AIDRPG track enemy groups?

How does it avoid turning “some enemies died” into “all enemies died”?

How do party members affect scenes?

What is the difference between party members, companions, followers, and summons?

How does the system prevent hundreds of NPCs or summons from becoming card spam?

How does scale affect plausibility?

How does AIDRPG handle one player versus a mob, party versus squad, army versus monster, or multi-protagonist stories?

The central rule is:

AIDRPG should track group-scale truth in summarized form unless an individual becomes important enough to deserve separate memory.

⸻

2. Core philosophy

AI Dungeon often writes group scenes loosely.

Examples:

* “A dozen goblins rush from the trees.”
* “Several guards fall.”
* “The rest scatter.”
* “Your companions hold the line.”
* “The militia surges forward.”
* “Three wolves circle you.”
* “Your summoned ravens tear at their eyes.”
* “The army clashes with the horde.”

Without group rules, the script can make bad assumptions.

Bad assumptions include:

* “several die” means all died
* “the rest scatter” means everyone died
* every goblin becomes an actor card
* every summoned creature becomes a card
* every villager becomes a follower
* absent companions are assumed present
* one ally’s injury applies to the player
* one protagonist’s items merge with another’s
* an army becomes one normal actor
* a crowd becomes permanent bloat

Chunk 8 exists to prevent that.

⸻

3. Ownership boundaries

Group-scale truth is split across owners.

PartyReducer owns

* party members
* companions in party state
* followers
* follower groups
* summons
* summon groups
* present/absent/separated party state
* party join/leave
* party availability
* party roles
* active party summary

ActorReducer owns

* named actor identity
* companion identity
* important individual NPCs
* major enemies
* recurring rivals
* named summons if persistent/important

ScaleReducer owns

* enemy groups
* ally groups
* battlefield groups
* group totals
* remaining/killed/fled/captured/routed counts
* reinforcement state
* large-scale conflict summaries
* scale tier
* multi-protagonist state
* group combat resolution

PlaceReducer owns

* current scene
* local hazards
* local exits
* battlefield location
* place-based group positions if needed

ProgressionReducer owns

* XP from group combat or group-scale events

ReputationReducer owns

* public/faction/social consequences of group-scale events

CardSync owns

* cards only after state is stable and important

The rule remains:

Group systems may propose events for each other, but each owner writes its own truth.

⸻

4. Group records

Any meaningful group should be represented as a group record instead of many individual actors unless individuals matter.

A group record should describe:

* group ID
* display name
* type
* side
* total count, if known
* remaining count, if known
* killed count
* wounded count
* fled count
* captured count
* routed count
* unknown/unresolved count
* leader, if known
* threat tier
* morale
* location/scene
* status
* resolved or unresolved
* source/evidence
* last updated turn

Example group:

A dozen goblins ambush the player.

Group state:

* display name: goblin ambushers
* type: enemy group
* total: 12
* remaining: 12
* killed: 0
* wounded: 0
* fled: 0
* captured: 0
* routed: 0
* threat tier: 2 each, group threat higher
* status: active
* resolved: false

If later the output says:

“You cut down three goblins, and two more flee into the trees.”

Then group state becomes:

* total: 12
* killed: 3
* fled: 2
* remaining active: 7
* resolved: false

The system must not treat the group as defeated.

⸻

5. Known count versus unknown count

Groups may have known or unknown size.

Known-count group

Example:

“Thirteen goblins attack.”

The system knows total = 13.

Casualty math should preserve remaining count.

If 9 die, 4 remain.

This exact case is a required regression expectation from your prior safeguard list: 13 goblins attack, 9 die → 4 remain.

Unknown-count group

Example:

“Several goblins attack.”

The system does not know total.

It should avoid exact math unless later clarified.

Possible representation:

* total: unknown
* active estimate: several
* killed: 2 if explicit
* remaining: unknown
* status: active unless clearly resolved

If the output says “the rest flee,” the group can resolve as routed/fled.

If the output says “several fall,” it should not assume all died.

⸻

6. Group casualty interpretation table

This table controls how loose narration should be interpreted.

“One falls”

Meaning:

* killed or downed count +1, depending wording
* group still active unless total was 1 or resolution confirmed

“Two die”

Meaning:

* killed +2
* remaining decreases by 2 if total known

“Several fall”

Meaning:

* casualties occurred
* exact count uncertain unless context gives number
* group likely still active
* may estimate 2–4 only if the system supports estimated counts

“Many fall”

Meaning:

* significant casualties
* not all unless explicitly stated
* morale may drop
* remaining unknown or reduced

“Half are cut down”

Meaning:

* killed/wounded about half
* if total known, calculate half
* if total unknown, record heavy casualties

“Nine die”

Meaning:

* killed +9
* if total known, remaining = total - killed - fled - captured - routed

“The rest scatter”

Meaning:

* unresolved active enemies become fled/routed
* not killed
* group may resolve as routed

“They flee”

Meaning:

* group status becomes fled or retreating
* not dead
* may still return later if important

“They surrender”

Meaning:

* remaining active enemies become captured/surrendered
* not dead
* custody/social/legal consequences may apply

“All fall”

Meaning:

* all active enemies defeated if source is direct and context supports it
* if enemy group is overwhelming, boss-like, or vague, validation may soften

“The last one drops”

Meaning:

* group can resolve if previous counts support only one remaining

“You cut through them”

Meaning:

* pressure or casualties
* not automatic full defeat unless explicit

“The battlefield goes silent”

Meaning:

* possible resolution
* needs context: dead, fled, hidden, or stunned?

“No goblins remain standing”

Meaning:

* active combatants defeated
* may be dead, unconscious, fled, or downed depending wording

The key rule:

Downed, fled, routed, captured, and killed are different states.

⸻

7. Group status categories

A group can be:

Active

Still fighting or threatening.

Pressured

Under attack, losing morale, but not resolved.

Wounded / reduced

Some members are wounded or killed.

Routed

Group cohesion breaks. They may flee, scatter, or stop fighting.

Fled

Group left the combat area.

Captured / surrendered

Group is no longer hostile because it surrendered or was restrained.

Hidden / dispersed

Group is not actively visible but may still exist.

Reinforced

New members join.

Resolved

No immediate group threat remains.

Resolved does not always mean dead.

A resolved group may be:

* killed
* fled
* captured
* surrendered
* dispersed
* driven off
* neutralized
* escaped

This matters for quests, loot, reputation, and future encounters.

⸻

8. Group threat scaling

A group is more dangerous than one member.

Group threat should consider:

* individual threat tier
* count
* coordination
* morale
* terrain
* surprise
* leader
* ranged weapons
* magic
* formation
* player’s area abilities
* party support
* chokepoints
* escape routes

Example:

One goblin may be Tier 2.

Ten goblins may become a Tier 4 or Tier 5 group threat if coordinated.

A narrow bridge may reduce their group advantage.

An open field may increase it.

A fireball may counter large numbers if the player has the MP and spell rank.

A shield wall may be far more dangerous than scattered enemies.

⸻

9. Group matchup labels

Group combat uses the same general matchup labels from Chunk 7, but with count and coordination included.

Labels:

* player overwhelms group
* player favored
* slightly favored
* even
* dangerous
* player disadvantaged
* heavily outmatched
* group overwhelms
* impossible without leverage

Examples:

A level 8 warrior versus three weak goblins:

* player favored or player overwhelms group

A level 2 novice versus thirteen goblins in open ground:

* heavily outmatched

A level 5 fire mage versus a swarm of weak insects with enough MP:

* player favored if area fire applies

A level 5 fire mage with 0 MP versus the same swarm:

* dangerous or disadvantaged

A lone player versus an army:

* impossible without leverage

⸻

10. Area effects and group damage

Some abilities or tactics affect multiple enemies.

Examples:

* fireball
* shockwave
* sweeping strike
* caltrops
* collapsing bridge
* poison cloud
* fear effect
* command shout
* trap
* landslide
* summoned swarm

Area effects should consider:

* ability rank
* cost
* range
* terrain
* enemy spacing
* enemy resistance
* friendly fire
* group size
* overkill risk
* environmental consequences

An area effect may produce:

* killed count
* wounded count
* routed count
* scattered count
* morale drop
* hazard creation
* resource cost
* collateral damage

Area effects should not automatically wipe large groups unless the effect is strong enough and the story supports it.

⸻

11. Party members

Party members are characters currently traveling, fighting, or acting with the player.

Party members are not all equal.

Types:

* companion
* temporary ally
* hired guard
* follower
* summon
* pet/familiar
* worker escort
* squad member
* multi-protagonist ally

PartyReducer owns whether they are present, absent, separated, captured, dead, dismissed, waiting, summoned, or following.

ActorReducer owns their identity if they are named/important.

Example:

Mara is an actor.

Mara currently traveling with the player is party state.

If Mara leaves to scout ahead:

* ActorReducer still owns Mara’s identity.
* PartyReducer marks Mara absent/scouting.
* Context should not assume Mara is standing beside the player.

⸻

12. Companion rules

Companions are persistent named allies with personal continuity.

They should track:

* identity
* role
* relationship
* loyalty/trust/fear
* current status
* current location
* party presence
* injuries/conditions if relevant
* major memories
* personal quest links
* availability
* combat role

Companions should not be created from every friendly NPC.

A friendly innkeeper is not automatically a companion.

A quest helper who travels with the player might become a temporary party member.

A recurring ally with personal connection may become a companion.

Companions should have stronger protection against casual deletion, random death, or name confusion than generic actors.

If AI Dungeon casually says “Mara vanishes forever” from weak narration, the system should validate carefully.

⸻

13. Temporary allies

Temporary allies are NPCs who help for a scene, quest, battle, or short duration.

Examples:

* two guards help in a fight
* a villager guides the player to the shrine
* a hunter assists against wolves
* a priest supports a ritual
* a caravan guard joins for one road

Temporary allies should usually be stored as:

* temporary party entry
* scene ally
* ally group
* actor link if named

They should not always become companions or generated actor cards.

Temporary ally state should expire or resolve when:

* scene ends
* quest step ends
* ally leaves
* ally dies
* ally is promoted
* time passes significantly

⸻

14. Followers

Followers are people, creatures, workers, retainers, disciples, troops, or supporters attached to the player but not necessarily equal companions.

Examples:

* five hired guards
* three apprentices
* ten villagers following orders
* a wolf pack loyal to the player
* guild recruits
* militia squad
* worker crew
* caravan hands

Followers should usually be group records.

Follower records should track:

* count
* type
* role
* loyalty
* morale
* current location
* current order
* status
* payment/contract if any
* leader/foreman if any
* availability
* casualties if combat happens

Followers should not become full actor cards individually unless a member becomes named and important.

Example:

“five hired guards” should be one follower group.

“Captain Jora, leader of the hired guards” can be an actor.

⸻

15. Summons

Summons are creatures, spirits, constructs, projections, familiars, magical allies, or called entities created by ability, ritual, item, contract, or story event.

Summons can be:

* temporary
* persistent
* named
* grouped
* controlled
* semi-controlled
* independent
* bound
* unstable

Summon records should track:

* summoner
* summon type
* count
* name if any
* duration
* control level
* loyalty
* current order
* cost
* maintenance
* location
* status
* dismissal condition
* death/banishment condition

Summons should not persist forever unless confirmed.

Summons should not duplicate from repeated output.

Summons should not create one card per minor creature.

Example:

“three shadow wolves” should be a summon group.

“Umbra, your bound shadow wolf” may become a named actor/companion if persistent and important.

⸻

16. Summon lifecycle

Summons should follow a lifecycle.

Attempted

The player tries to summon.

No summon exists yet.

Failed

The attempt fails.

May create ability evidence, MP cost, or backlash.

Manifesting

The summon begins forming but is unstable.

May be interrupted.

Active

The summon exists and can act.

Sustained

The summon remains through cost, concentration, duration, or bond.

Unstable

The summon may disobey, fade, weaken, or backlash.

Dismissed

The summon is intentionally ended.

Destroyed / banished

The summon is defeated or forced away.

Persistent

The summon remains as familiar, companion, bound spirit, construct, or permanent ally.

Persistent summons require strong story support.

⸻

17. Summon control rules

Summons should not always obey perfectly.

Control depends on:

* ability rank
* summon type
* cost paid
* ritual quality
* relationship
* intelligence
* bond
* enemy interference
* summoner condition
* MP/EP availability
* command clarity
* story constraints

Control levels:

Full control

Summon obeys direct commands reliably.

Usually requires stable ability, strong bond, or low-level summon.

Guided control

Summon follows general intent but may improvise.

Independent ally

Summon helps but has its own will.

Unstable control

Summon may misbehave, fade, lash out, or ignore orders.

Hostile/rebellious

Summon becomes a threat.

This should require failed/unsafe summoning or story support.

⸻

18. Party contribution in combat

Party members can influence combat, but should not erase challenge automatically.

Party contribution depends on:

* who is present
* role
* health
* loyalty
* equipment
* ability
* morale
* coordination
* terrain
* enemy focus
* player commands
* current threat
* surprise
* injuries

Party members may:

* attack enemies
* defend player
* heal
* distract
* hold position
* protect civilians
* carry items
* scout
* trigger traps
* be injured
* be captured
* flee
* disagree
* die if strongly validated

Party help should be reflected in matchup.

Example:

A lone level 3 player versus six bandits may be disadvantaged.

A level 3 player with two capable companions and a chokepoint may be even or favored.

⸻

19. Party presence rules

The system must track who is actually present.

A party member can be:

* present
* nearby
* scouting
* guarding camp
* waiting elsewhere
* separated
* missing
* captured
* dead
* dismissed
* unavailable
* summoned
* expired

Context should only include party members relevant to the current scene.

If a companion is absent, AI Dungeon should not narrate them acting beside the player unless the story brings them back.

Example:

Mara went to scout the north road.

Context should say:

“Mara is scouting ahead and is not currently beside you.”

Not:

“Mara stands next to you.”

⸻

20. Party injury and death rules

Party members can be injured or killed, but validation should be careful.

Named companions need stronger evidence than generic allies.

Weak narration should not casually erase important companions.

Example weak output:

“Mara is probably dead.”

Result:

* pending danger or rumor
* not confirmed death

Example strong output:

“The spear pierces Mara’s chest. She collapses, blood pooling beneath her, and does not breathe.”

Result:

* death-boundary or death candidate
* still validated according to importance, setting, and possible healing/protection

Generic follower death can be tracked by count.

Example:

“Two of your hired guards fall.”

Follower group:

* killed +2
* remaining count decreases

Do not create individual death records unless they are named/important.

⸻

21. Morale

Groups and parties should have morale.

Morale affects:

* whether enemies flee
* whether followers obey
* whether workers continue
* whether allies hold the line
* whether summons destabilize
* whether mobs rout
* whether companions panic or stand firm

Morale states:

* fearless
* steady
* pressured
* shaken
* broken
* routed

Morale changes from:

* casualties
* leader death
* intimidation
* magic fear
* player reputation
* overwhelming threat
* victory momentum
* betrayal
* terrain disadvantage
* rallying speech
* leadership domain

Morale should be especially important for groups.

A group can be defeated by routing, not just killing.

⸻

22. Scale tiers

Scale describes the size and scope of action.

Scale 0 — Personal

One person’s immediate action.

Example:

Player picks lock.

Scale 1 — Duel

One-on-one or very small conflict.

Example:

Player duels a guard.

Scale 2 — Small group

Several participants.

Example:

Player and companion fight three wolves.

Scale 3 — Skirmish

Small squads or packs.

Example:

Ten goblins ambush the party.

Scale 4 — Mob / unit

Crowds, militia groups, large monster packs.

Example:

A village mob surrounds the player.

Scale 5 — Battlefield

Dozens or hundreds in organized conflict.

Example:

Militia and raiders clash.

Scale 6 — Domain conflict

Holdings, armies, settlements, major resources.

Example:

A siege against the player’s keep.

Scale 7 — Regional conflict

Factions, armies, monsters, disasters affecting regions.

Scale 8 — World-scale conflict

Wars, gods, ancient powers, cataclysms.

Scale affects:

* what counts as plausible
* what records are needed
* whether individuals are summarized
* what Context includes
* whether CardSync mirrors the event
* whether domain/faction systems are involved

⸻

23. Scale compression

The larger the scale, the more the system should summarize.

Personal scale:

* specific injuries/items/actions matter

Small group scale:

* named participants matter
* group counts matter

Skirmish scale:

* group states matter more than every individual

Battlefield scale:

* units, morale, objectives, casualties, and commanders matter

Domain scale:

* holdings, supplies, projects, armies, and strategic outcomes matter

World scale:

* only major factions, forces, places, and outcomes should be tracked

Rule:

Do not create individual actor records for every soldier in a battle.

Do not create item records for every dropped spear.

Do not create cards for every unit.

Summarize unless something becomes important.

⸻

24. Multi-protagonist support

Multi-protagonist support means the story may include more than one playable or viewpoint character.

This is different from normal companions.

A protagonist has their own:

* identity
* stats
* resources
* inventory
* abilities
* injuries
* location
* active scene
* relationships
* quests or objectives
* card/context summary if important

Multi-protagonist rules:

* do not merge inventories
* do not apply one protagonist’s injury to another
* do not assume all protagonists share location
* track active viewpoint
* Context should focus on current protagonist
* inactive protagonists should be summarized only when relevant
* party state can link protagonists when together
* split-party state must be allowed

ScaleReducer owns multi-protagonist state.

PlayerReducer may own the active player sheet depending on final implementation, but the design must not assume only one body exists if multi-protagonist mode is active.

⸻

25. Multi-protagonist boundaries

Multi-protagonist support should probably be advanced-mode.

Default mode:

* one primary player
* party members and companions tracked separately

Advanced mode:

* multiple protagonists
* active protagonist switching
* separate sheets
* split scenes
* separate inventories/resources

The system should not overcomplicate v1 if the story does not need it.

But the architecture should avoid hardcoding assumptions that make multi-protagonist impossible later.

⸻

26. Group and party Context behavior

Context should include group and party facts only when relevant.

Good Context examples:

“The party is fighting seven remaining goblins in the ravine. Three goblins are dead, two fled, and the rest look shaken.”

“Mara is with you and wounded in the shoulder. Harlan is guarding the camp and is not present.”

“You currently command five hired guards. They are loyal but shaken after losing two members.”

“Your three shadow wolves are active but unstable and will fade soon unless sustained.”

Bad Context examples:

Dumping every group counter every turn when no group scene is active.

Listing every follower in a 30-person workforce.

Treating absent companions as present.

Injecting dead enemies as active.

Context should prioritize:

* current active group threats
* present companions
* active summons
* relevant followers
* unresolved group counts
* immediate morale/rout/capture states

⸻

27. Group and party Story Card behavior

Cards should be rare and stable.

Do not generate cards for:

* every enemy group
* every temporary ally
* every summon
* every hired worker
* every goblin
* every crowd
* every battle state

Cards may be appropriate for:

* major companion
* persistent follower organization
* named summon/familiar
* major enemy group/faction
* important party summary
* major army/unit tied to domain
* recurring villain group
* major battlefield/domain conflict

Protected summary cards may include:

* Party & Companions
* Domain & Contracts
* Scale & Protagonists, if needed

Generated actor cards may exist for important companions or named summons.

Generated domain/faction cards may summarize major units or holdings.

CardSync mirrors stable state only.

⸻

28. Group combat validation rules

Accept group casualties if

* number is explicit
* source is direct narration
* target group is clear
* damage source is plausible
* ability/tactic supports scale
* duplicate operation not already applied

Example:

“Your fireball kills four goblins.”

Accept:

* group killed +4
* MP cost
* possible morale drop

Reject or soften casualties if

* number exceeds remaining count
* attack cannot plausibly affect that many
* source is rumor/dream/quote
* group identity unclear
* duplicate output
* enemy has known immunity
* result is impossible without leverage

Example:

“Your dagger swing kills all fifty soldiers.”

If no leverage:

* reject or soften to “one soldier wounded” or “you create brief panic” depending output and validation rules

Accept rout if

* narration says enemies flee/scatter/break
* morale context supports it
* casualties or intimidation support it
* leader defeat supports it

Distinguish rout from kill

If enemies flee, they are not dead.

They may return.

They may spread rumors.

They may abandon loot.

They may regroup.

Accept companion aid if

* companion is present
* companion has plausible ability/equipment
* action fits their role
* output directly includes their action

Reject companion action if

* companion is absent
* companion is incapacitated
* companion is dead
* companion lacks required ability/equipment
* output contradicts known state

Accept summon action if

* summon exists
* summon is active
* duration/control allows action
* action fits summon type
* cost/maintenance is handled

Reject summon action if

* summon expired
* summon never manifested
* summon is absent
* summon type cannot perform action
* control failed

⸻

29. Scale validation examples

Example 1 — Thirteen goblins

Output:

“Thirteen goblins rush from the trees.”

State:

Enemy group created.

* total 13
* remaining 13
* active

Later output:

“Nine goblins fall beneath your party’s counterattack.”

State:

* killed +9
* remaining 4
* group still active unless routed/resolved

Later output:

“The last four break and run.”

State:

* fled +4
* remaining active 0
* resolved as routed/fled, not all killed

Example 2 — Several guards

Output:

“Several guards block the gate.”

State:

* group created with unknown count
* active estimate: several

Output:

“You cut two down.”

State:

* killed +2
* remaining unknown
* group still active unless text confirms otherwise

Example 3 — Companion absent

State:

Mara is scouting ahead.

Output:

“Mara blocks the arrow beside you.”

Validation:

* reject or flag contradiction unless output also establishes Mara returned
* possible actor.location update only if arrival is clearly confirmed

Example 4 — Summon expired

State:

Shadow wolves expired last turn.

Output:

“The shadow wolves tear into the bandits.”

Validation:

* reject unless new summon event occurs
* possible hallucinated/stale Context issue

Example 5 — Army battle

Output:

“The militia clashes with the raiders outside the wall.”

State:

* militia group
* raider group
* battlefield scale
* morale/casualty summaries

Do not create 100 actor records.

Track units, leaders, casualties, morale, and domain/faction consequences.

⸻

30. Progression from group and party scenes

Group scenes can reward progression, but carefully.

Progression depends on:

* player contribution
* party contribution
* enemy threat
* risk
* scale
* leadership
* tactics
* survival
* cost
* objective completion

Examples:

If the party defeats a wolf pack and the player meaningfully fought:

* Level XP
* relevant Stat XP
* relevant Domain XP
* possible party/actor memory

If followers defeat enemies while player only watches:

* reduced player XP
* leadership/domain XP if player commanded them
* reputation or quest progress if appropriate

If army wins battle because of domain preparation:

* domain/leadership progression
* reputation/faction effects
* not necessarily direct ATK XP

If summon kills enemies:

* ability growth for summoning/control
* Magic Domain XP
* less direct ATK XP unless player contributed physically

Rule:

XP should match what the player actually did.

⸻

31. Loot from groups

Group loot should be summarized.

Do not add every enemy’s gear automatically.

Examples:

After defeating goblin group:

Possible available loot:

* crude weapons
* small coin pouch
* arrows
* worn leather scraps

But inventory gains require confirmed taking/searching.

Output:

“You search the fallen goblins and gather 6 copper and a crude dagger.”

Accepted:

* coins.gain 6 copper
* item.gain crude dagger

Do not automatically add:

* thirteen goblin swords
* thirteen sets of armor
* thirteen ears
* every dropped object

Unless the player explicitly collects them and carrying capacity/context supports it.

⸻

32. Party rewards and costs

Party members and followers can also be affected by consequences.

Possible party consequences:

* companion injury
* companion fatigue
* follower casualties
* summon expiry
* morale loss
* loyalty gain/loss
* relationship change
* companion ability growth
* follower group experience
* contract breach
* wage expectation
* reputation change

But avoid over-simulating every ally unless important.

Named companions deserve more detail.

Follower groups should use summarized state.

Temporary allies may resolve after the scene.

⸻

33. Leadership and command

Commanding others should use:

* Leadership Domain
* Social Domain
* INT
* LCK for risky morale moments
* reputation
* relationship
* authority
* payment/contract
* fear/loyalty
* current morale
* clarity of order

Commands may:

* improve coordination
* prevent rout
* focus attacks
* protect civilians
* trigger follower action
* create domain/labor progress
* cause obedience or refusal

Followers should not obey impossible orders just because the player says them.

Example:

“Five frightened villagers charge the dragon.”

Likely result:

* refusal
* morale collapse
* maybe one reckless casualty
* no clean success without extraordinary leverage

⸻

34. Friendly fire and collateral damage

Group and scale scenes should allow collateral consequences.

Examples:

* fireball harms allies
* collapsing bridge kills enemies and blocks route
* summoned beast damages property
* panicked crowd tramples civilians
* militia victory burns a field
* area spell destroys loot

Collateral should be validated and not overused.

It matters for:

* reputation
* domain
* contracts
* quests
* place memory
* actor memory
* inventory/loot
* future hazards

⸻

35. Scale and realism

Scale must prevent absurd outcomes while allowing heroic moments.

AIDRPG should ask:

* Can this action affect this many targets?
* Does the player have area capability?
* Is there terrain leverage?
* Are enemies clustered?
* Is there a leader or morale break?
* Is this a symbolic victory, tactical victory, or full destruction?
* Is the group actually resolved?
* Are there survivors?
* Are there witnesses?
* Is there collateral damage?

Example:

A player cannot normally “stab the army to death.”

But the player might:

* kill the commander
* break morale
* collapse the bridge
* ignite stored oil
* open the gate for allies
* rally defenders
* assassinate a mage controlling the army
* use legendary area magic
* survive long enough for reinforcements

This keeps scale scenes playable without making them impossible.

36. Safety rules

36.1 No group wipe from vague language

“Several fall” does not mean the group is defeated.

36.2 No count loss beyond total

If 13 enemies exist, casualties cannot exceed 13 unless reinforcements or count correction happens.

36.3 No absent companion action

A companion cannot act in the current scene if absent unless the output clearly brings them back.

36.4 No summon persistence without support

Temporary summons expire unless maintained or confirmed permanent.

36.5 No card spam

Groups, followers, and summons are summarized unless important.

36.6 No individual bloat

Do not create actor records for every generic member.

36.7 No party merge

Companion, follower, summon, and protagonist states must stay separate.

36.8 No free army control

Followers and armies require morale, loyalty, payment, authority, command, and context.

36.9 No duplicate casualties

Repeated output does not apply group losses twice.

36.10 No XP for allies doing everything unless player contribution supports it

Reward should match player action.

39. Final Chunk 8 doctrine

Groups are not piles of individual actors.

Groups are summarized truth.

Known counts must be respected.

Casualties, fleeing, routing, surrendering, and capture are different outcomes.

Party members must be tracked by presence.

Companions are persistent named allies, not every friendly NPC.

Followers are usually groups.

Summons have duration, control, cost, and lifecycle.

Scale changes what is plausible.

Large scenes should be compressed.

Actor cards are for important individuals, not every soldier.

Group cards are rare and only for stable important entities.

Player rewards should match player contribution.

Duplicate outputs cannot duplicate casualties.

The goal is to let AI Dungeon run large, exciting scenes without losing track of who is present, who remains, who fled, who died, and what actually changed.
