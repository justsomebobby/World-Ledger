AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 6: Ability, Skill, Talent, Passive, and Specialization Lifecycle

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 4 defined the progression economy.

Chunk 5 defined stats, resources, recovery, and scaling basics.

Chunk 6 defines how capabilities work.

This chunk answers:

What is an ability?

What is an ability candidate?

What is the difference between a skill, talent, passive, trait, and specialization?

How does a new power become real?

How does a failed attempt still matter?

How do ability costs become known?

How do abilities improve over time?

How do abilities stay believable instead of becoming random power bloat?

How does the system stop fake, pretend, quoted, or failed abilities from becoming canon?

The core ownership rule from Chunk 2 still applies:

AbilityReducer owns ability candidates, abilities, skills, talents, passives, ability evidence, ability lifecycle stage, ability costs, ability limits, reliability, aliases, refinement, and merge/split records.

ProgressionReducer owns Level XP, Domain XP, and Stat Training XP that may result from ability use.

CardSync only mirrors stable/card-eligible abilities. It does not create abilities.

⸻

2. Core ability philosophy

Abilities should feel discovered through play.

They should not appear just because a phrase sounded cool.

AIDRPG should let the player experiment, fail, train, refine, and eventually stabilize powers in a way that feels like an RPG but still fits AI Dungeon’s freeform narration.

The system should support:

* ordinary skills
* martial techniques
* spells
* supernatural powers
* passive traits
* learned talents
* racial features
* mutations
* divine blessings
* cursed abilities
* item-linked abilities
* temporary powers
* unstable abilities
* mastered signature abilities

But all of them must obey canon law:

Text proposes.

Validator judges.

AbilityReducer records.

ProgressionReducer rewards related growth.

Context reports relevant capability.

CardSync mirrors only stable/card-eligible records.

⸻

3. Ability categories

AIDRPG should distinguish several capability categories.

These categories are not all separate systems. They are different record types owned by AbilityReducer or related body/build systems.

3.1 Ability

An ability is the broad parent category.

It means the player has some structured capability beyond ordinary description.

Examples:

* Mana Slash
* Shadow Step
* Flame Blade
* Iron Skin
* Beast Speech
* Healing Light
* Rune Sense
* Blood Echo
* Stormcall
* Silent Footwork

An ability record may later be classified as a skill, talent, passive, or special feature.

3.2 Skill

A skill is an active, intentional technique.

The player chooses to use it.

Examples:

* Mana Slash
* Fireball
* Backstab
* Shield Bash
* Lockpick
* Healing Touch
* Quick Draw
* Wind Step
* Arcane Ward

Skills usually have:

* activation method
* cost
* effect
* reliability
* limits
* failure modes
* improvement path

Skills should often cost MP, EP, time, tools, materials, or opportunity.

A skill is not always-on.

3.3 Talent

A talent is a trained style, form, stance, art, method, or aptitude that shapes how the player performs actions.

Examples:

* Wolf Guard Stance
* Duelist’s Rhythm
* Silent Footwork
* Iron Body Training
* Rune-Smithing Method
* Beast-Tamer Instinct
* Flameblade Style
* Herbalist’s Eye

Talents may be active, semi-active, or style-based.

They usually modify a family of actions rather than producing one discrete effect.

A talent might not have a direct “cast cost,” but it may require stance, focus, training, equipment, or stamina.

3.4 Passive

A passive is an always-on or conditionally-on capability.

Examples:

* Night Vision
* Fire Resistance
* Poison Tolerance
* Undead Endurance
* Draconic Scales
* Mana Sensitivity
* Battle Instinct
* Fast Recovery
* Silent Breathing

Passives usually do not require activation.

But passives can still have limits.

Fire Resistance may reduce fire damage, not make the player immune to lava.

Night Vision may help in darkness, not against magical blindness.

Fast Recovery may improve healing, not instantly erase severed limbs.

3.5 Trait

A trait is a persistent character quality, adaptation, flaw, mutation, blessing, curse, scar, or identity-linked fact.

Examples:

* Scarred by Dragonfire
* Cursed Blood
* Fae-Touched
* Hollow-Boned
* Iron Stomach
* Mark of the Moon
* One-Eyed
* Oathbound
* Undead Body

Traits can overlap with passives.

The distinction should be:

A trait describes what the character is or has become.

A passive describes what the character can consistently do because of it.

Example:

Trait: Draconic Scales.

Passive: Fire Resistance.

Trait ownership may belong to BodyReducer if it is primarily body state, or AbilityReducer if it functions as a capability. The guide should allow both, but avoid duplicate truth.

3.6 Specialization

A specialization is a long-term focus derived from repeated play.

Examples:

* Duelist
* Fire Mage
* Shield Fighter
* Monster Hunter
* Investigator
* Alchemist
* Beast Tamer
* Commander
* Assassin
* Healer
* Rune Smith

Specialization should usually be derived from evidence, not directly granted from one line.

A specialization describes the player’s developing style.

It should influence Context, training interpretation, ability plausibility, and reward routing.

It should not lock the player into a class.

⸻

4. Capability category decision table

Use this table when deciding what a new capability is.

If the player intentionally activates it to produce a specific effect, it is probably a skill.

If it shapes how the player fights, moves, crafts, investigates, or trains over many actions, it is probably a talent.

If it is always-on or conditionally-on without direct activation, it is probably a passive.

If it describes a permanent or persistent physical, magical, social, or spiritual quality, it is probably a trait.

If it describes the player’s long-term focus or role, it is probably a specialization.

Examples:

Mana Slash: skill.

Wolf Guard Stance: talent.

Night Vision: passive.

Scarred by Dragonfire: trait.

Monster Hunter: specialization.

Fire Resistance from Draconic Scales: passive linked to trait.

Duelist’s Rhythm: talent, possibly specialization evidence.

Healing Light: skill.

Fast Recovery: passive.

Cursed Blood: trait, possibly passive effects if defined.

5. Ability lifecycle

Abilities should move through stages.

This prevents one random phrase from becoming a fully reliable power.

Stage 0 — None

The ability does not exist.

There is no candidate and no evidence.

Example:

The player has never attempted Mana Slash.

Stage 1 — Hint

The story suggests a possible future capability, but no real attempt or manifestation has occurred.

Examples:

* “You feel something stir in your blood.”
* “The old book mentions flame-shaped mana.”
* “Mara says your stance resembles an old sword art.”

A hint may become pending lore or candidate seed.

It should not become a usable ability.

It should not get a card.

Stage 2 — Candidate

A candidate is a possible developing ability.

It exists because the player made a real attempt, experienced a partial manifestation, received training, or encountered repeated evidence.

Examples:

* Mana flickered once during a sword swing.
* The player tried to cast Mana Slash and felt a pulse of heat.
* A teacher identified the beginning of a technique.
* The player repeatedly moves silently without knowing why.

Candidate records should store:

* tentative name
* category guess
* intended effect
* evidence count
* source scenes
* failure notes
* cost clues
* domain/stat links
* confidence
* aliases
* current stage
* last attempt turn

Candidates are not reliable.

Candidates should appear in /abilityaudit.

They may appear in Context only if relevant.

They should not get generated ability cards yet.

Stage 3 — Soft-failed candidate

A soft-failed candidate is a candidate with real attempts but no stable success.

Example:

“You try to form Mana Slash, but nothing happens.”

This can still record:

* attempt count
* failure mode
* MP/EP cost
* domain XP proposal
* stat XP proposal
* evidence of what does not work

Soft failure should not erase the candidate unless repeated failures show it is invalid.

Soft failure should not stabilize the ability.

Stage 4 — Unstable manifestation

An unstable manifestation means the ability produced a partial, uncontrolled, weak, or inconsistent effect.

Examples:

* the blade sparks but collapses
* the player vanishes for one step and reappears dizzy
* healing light closes a tiny cut but drains too much MP
* the shield flares once under stress
* the player hears a beast’s intent but cannot understand words

This stage can create stronger evidence.

It may give more ability growth than total failure.

It still should not be treated as reliable.

Stage 5 — Partial success

Partial success means the ability works in a limited way.

Examples:

* Mana Slash cuts a shallow groove but fails to hold shape
* Shadow Step moves the player only a few feet
* Healing Light stops bleeding but does not heal the wound
* Fire Resistance reduces burns but does not prevent them

Partial success should update:

* effect understanding
* cost estimate
* limits
* reliability
* evidence
* possible skill rank

It still may not be fully stabilized.

Stage 6 — Stabilized ability

A stabilized ability is real and usable.

It has enough evidence or direct confirmation to become part of the stable ability list.

Stabilization may come from:

* repeated successful use
* explicit breakthrough
* teacher confirmation
* controlled activation
* story-granted power
* ritual completion
* item bond
* species awakening
* major milestone

A stabilized ability should have:

* stable name
* category
* effect summary
* activation method
* known or estimated cost
* limits
* reliability
* source/origin
* related domains/stats
* current rank
* aliases
* context priority
* card eligibility flag if important

Stabilized does not mean mastered.

It means real and usable.

Stage 7 — Refined ability

A refined ability has improved control, efficiency, precision, or application.

Examples:

* Mana Slash costs less MP
* Shadow Step can curve around an obstacle
* Healing Light can target poison symptoms
* Fire Resistance now applies to magical flame partly

Refinement should come from repeated use, training, teachers, breakthroughs, or special circumstances.

Refinement should update costs, limits, effect scope, reliability, or variants.

Stage 8 — Mastered ability

A mastered ability is a signature capability.

It is reliable under pressure and strongly tied to the player’s identity.

Examples:

* Mana Slash becomes a signature combat opener
* Silent Footwork allows near-effortless stealth in normal conditions
* Healing Light becomes precise and efficient
* Iron Body allows major impact resistance

Mastery should still obey:

* resource costs
* counters
* conditions
* scale limits
* environmental limits
* story logic

Mastery does not mean infinite free success.

Stage 9 — Evolved ability

An evolved ability changes into a stronger, broader, specialized, or variant form.

Examples:

* Mana Slash evolves into Crescent Mana Slash
* Shadow Step evolves into Shadow Thread
* Healing Light evolves into Lifeweave
* Wolf Guard Stance evolves into Moonfang Guard

Evolution should preserve history and meaning.

It should not randomly rename the ability into something unrelated.

It may create:

* variant ability
* upgraded form
* branch path
* specialization tag
* new cost profile
* new limits

Stage 10 — Archived / merged / deprecated

An old ability candidate or old name may be archived.

Examples:

* duplicate candidates merge
* old tentative name becomes alias
* failed false candidate expires
* old form becomes variant
* temporary power fades

Archive should preserve history without cluttering active Context.

6. Ability rank scale

Abilities, skills, and talents should have ranks.

Recommended rank scale:

Rank 0 — Unknown

No usable ability.

At most a hint.

Rank 1 — Attempted

The player has tried or shown the first signs.

Very unreliable.

High failure risk.

Costs unclear.

Rank 2 — Developing

Repeated attempts or weak manifestations exist.

Still unreliable.

Can appear in /abilityaudit.

Rarely appears in Context unless relevant.

Rank 3 — Basic

The ability can work in favorable conditions.

Costs are rough estimates.

Failure still possible under pressure.

Rank 4 — Practiced

The ability is usable in normal conditions.

Known limits begin to form.

Cost confidence improves.

Rank 5 — Reliable

The ability is stable and generally dependable within its limits.

Can be used as part of regular gameplay.

May be card-eligible if important.

Rank 6 — Refined

The ability becomes more efficient, precise, or flexible.

Failure risk decreases in normal conditions.

Rank 7 — Advanced

The ability can handle pressure, combination use, or harder targets.

Counters and costs still matter.

Rank 8 — Expert

The ability is a major part of the player’s build.

It can influence difficult scenes but not erase scale or story constraints.

Rank 9 — Master

The ability is signature-level.

It can justify major feats when resources and context support it.

Rank 10 — Legendary

The ability is mythic or campaign-defining.

It should require major story justification.

It still respects counters, costs, conditions, and scale.

⸻

7. What ability rank affects

Ability rank should affect:

* reliability
* cost efficiency
* strength of effect
* precision
* failure risk
* Context confidence
* validator bias
* progression reward quality
* possible variants
* card summary wording
* combat plausibility
* crafting/investigation/social plausibility if relevant

Ability rank should not automatically:

* guarantee success
* remove all costs
* bypass counters
* ignore injuries
* ignore resource state
* defeat impossible enemies without leverage
* solve quests automatically
* create cards by itself

Example:

Rank 5 Mana Slash is reliable against ordinary physical targets when the player has enough MP and a usable weapon.

It does not automatically cut through a divine barrier, an intangible ghost, or a mountain.

⸻

8. Ability evidence

Ability evidence is the record that supports a candidate or ability.

Evidence should include:

* real attempts
* partial manifestations
* confirmed successful uses
* teacher feedback
* observed costs
* observed limits
* failure patterns
* training scenes
* related domain/stat growth
* story origin
* conditions where it worked
* conditions where it failed

Evidence should not include:

* pretend actions
* jokes
* dreams unless dream powers are explicitly canon
* rumors
* quoted instructions
* card text
* debug examples
* impossible unsupported claims

Ability evidence should be weighted.

A clear successful use is stronger than a weak sensation.

A teacher-confirmed technique is stronger than a vague feeling.

Repeated soft failures can still be useful, but should not stabilize the ability by themselves unless they build toward partial manifestation.

⸻

9. Candidate creation rules

A candidate may be created when there is enough real evidence.

Create a candidate from:

* real attempt
* partial manifestation
* teacher identifies a technique
* repeated unusual behavior
* species/trait awakening
* item bond reaction
* ritual beginning
* confirmed story event suggesting power
* training directed at a specific capability

Do not create a candidate from:

* pretend use
* jokes
* quoted dialogue
* rumor
* dream/vision unless dream mechanics are canon
* one vague phrase with no action
* player saying “I have X now”
* card text
* debug text
* system explanation

Example accepted candidate:

“You focus mana through the blade. A faint blue edge flickers before collapsing.”

Candidate:

* Mana Slash
* evidence: unstable mana edge
* stage: unstable manifestation or developing
* not stable

Example rejected candidate:

“I pretend my sword fires a Mana Slash.”

Result:

* hard deny
* no candidate

⸻

10. Candidate stabilization rules

A candidate becomes stable only when enough support exists.

Stabilization can happen through any of these paths.

10.1 Repeated success path

The ability works several times in normal or stressful conditions.

Example:

Mana Slash succeeds in three separate validated scenes.

Result:

* candidate may stabilize

10.2 Breakthrough path

The story explicitly describes a breakthrough.

Example:

“After hours of correction, the mana finally locks into a clean crescent along your blade.”

Result:

* stabilization likely accepted

10.3 Teacher confirmation path

A qualified teacher confirms the ability.

Example:

Captain Jora nods. “That was the first true form of Mana Slash.”

Result:

* strong stabilization evidence

10.4 Ritual or item path

A ritual, artifact, bond, blessing, or transformation grants a defined ability.

Example:

“The rune burns into your palm, and you feel the warding spell settle into your bones.”

Result:

* ability may stabilize if the event is validated

10.5 Species or body awakening path

A species trait or body change creates a passive or ability.

Example:

“Your draconic scales harden, and the fire no longer burns as deeply.”

Result:

* trait/passive may stabilize

10.6 Crisis manifestation path

A power appears clearly under life-threatening stress.

Example:

“As the blade falls, shadows fold around you and you reappear behind the attacker.”

Result:

* strong candidate evidence or immediate stabilization depending on clarity and setting

Stabilization should not happen from:

* one failed attempt
* “nothing happens”
* pretend use
* quoted dialogue
* rumor
* dream
* unsupported player claim
* generic cool phrase
* card text

⸻

11. Ability cost model

Most active skills should have costs.

Costs can include:

* MP
* EP
* HP backlash
* time
* focus/concentration
* material component
* weapon requirement
* tool requirement
* cooldown
* environmental requirement
* condition requirement
* risk
* opportunity
* social or spiritual cost

11.1 MP costs

Use MP for:

* magic
* psychic powers
* spiritual techniques
* focus-heavy abilities
* rituals
* spell maintenance

11.2 EP costs

Use EP for:

* martial techniques
* movement abilities
* stances
* dodges
* physical skills
* endurance-based powers

11.3 Hybrid costs

Use MP and EP for:

* mana-infused sword techniques
* body-enhancement magic
* supernatural movement
* spellblade techniques
* ritual combat arts

Example:

Mana Slash may cost MP to shape mana and EP to swing/control the technique under pressure.

11.4 HP costs

Use HP costs rarely.

Appropriate for:

* blood magic
* overcasting
* cursed powers
* severe backlash
* self-sacrifice abilities
* dangerous transformations

HP cost should never be casually applied without clear support.

11.5 Material costs

Use for:

* rituals
* crafting abilities
* alchemy
* runes
* ammunition-like powers
* consumable talismans

11.6 Time costs

Use for:

* rituals
* crafting techniques
* meditation
* large spells
* domain abilities
* investigation methods
* recovery abilities

⸻

12. Cost discovery

Ability costs should not need to be perfectly known at first.

Early candidates may have uncertain costs.

The ability record can store:

* estimated cost
* observed cost range
* cost confidence
* resource type
* overuse risk
* failure cost
* maintenance cost
* scaling cost
* known cost modifiers

Cost confidence should increase through repeated confirmed use.

Example:

First Mana Slash attempt:

* cost unknown
* MP strain observed

After several uses:

* estimated cost: moderate MP, light EP
* confidence: medium

After repeated stable use:

* cost: 8 MP + 3 EP
* confidence: high

The existing ability-cost work already included cost guesses, resource guesses, observed costs, cost confidence, and ability card updates over time.  ￼

⸻

13. Cost scaling

Costs should scale with:

* ability rank
* effect size
* target difficulty
* distance
* duration
* precision
* pressure
* injury state
* resource state
* environmental support
* enemy resistance
* overcharging
* multi-target use

Low rank

Costs are inefficient.

Failure risk is high.

Overuse is dangerous.

Mid rank

Costs become predictable.

Reliability improves.

Normal use is manageable.

High rank

Costs may decrease, or effect per cost may improve.

The ability may handle harder situations.

Overcharged use

Costs increase sharply.

Failure may cause backlash, exhaustion, injury, condition, or instability.

⸻

14. Failure modes

Abilities should have meaningful failure modes.

Common failure modes:

* fizzles
* weak effect
* wrong target
* partial effect
* backlash
* resource drain
* fatigue
* injury
* condition
* alerting enemies
* damaging equipment
* creating unstable side effect
* loss of concentration
* delayed activation
* temporary lockout

Failure mode should match the ability.

Examples:

Failed fire spell:

* MP loss
* smoke
* burn risk
* no stable flame

Failed teleport:

* short displacement
* dizziness
* EP/MP loss
* arrival off target
* possible danger if severe

Failed healing:

* MP loss
* pain reduced but wound remains
* bleeding slows but does not stop
* healer fatigue

Failed stealth talent:

* noise made
* guard alerted
* EP cost
* no invisibility effect

Failure should be fun when possible.

It should create story consequence, not just “nothing.”

⸻

15. Soft failure and learning

A real failed ability attempt can still teach.

Soft failure may produce:

* evidence
* cost observation
* failure pattern
* domain XP
* stat XP
* fatigue
* clue about required condition
* clue about missing resource
* teacher correction
* partial progress

Soft failure should not produce:

* stable ability
* full success
* generated ability card
* major power jump
* free mastery

Example:

“You try to form Mana Slash, but the mana scatters because your grip slips.”

Possible learning:

* evidence: needs stable grip
* cost: small MP
* stat link: ATK/INT
* domain: Magic/Melee
* no stable ability

⸻

16. Hard denial rules

Hard denial blocks false abilities.

Hard-deny ability events from:

* pretend actions
* jokes
* quotes
* rumors
* dreams unless dream magic is canon
* fake examples
* debug text
* player simply declaring mastery
* “nothing happens” being parsed as success
* failed attempts being parsed as stable
* card text being parsed back as ability truth
* impossible unsupported power jumps

Examples:

“I pretend to cast Dragonfire.”

Result:

* hard deny
* no candidate

“She says, ‘You use Shadow Step.’”

Result:

* hard deny or reject ability success
* no ability progress unless actual use occurs

“You fail to summon the wolf spirit.”

Result:

* no summon
* possible soft-failed attempt if real effort occurred
* no stable summon ability

⸻

17. Ability limits

Every ability should have limits.

Limits make abilities fun and believable.

Limit categories:

* range
* duration
* target type
* resource cost
* cooldown
* concentration
* environmental need
* item/weapon requirement
* line of sight
* emotional state
* physical condition
* domain rank
* rank threshold
* enemy resistance
* scale limit
* risk/backlash
* moral/spiritual condition

Example:

Mana Slash limits:

* requires bladed weapon or focused mana edge
* costs MP and some EP
* works best at melee/short range
* weak against anti-magic barriers
* less reliable when exhausted
* stronger with Melee/Magic domain growth

Example:

Night Vision limits:

* helps in natural darkness
* may not pierce magical darkness
* does not reveal invisible creatures
* bright flashes may cause pain if species supports it

Limits should be updated through confirmed use.

⸻

18. Ability aliases and naming

Abilities may have aliases.

Example:

* Mana Slash
* Mana Edge
* Blue Crescent
* Crescent Cut

The system should preserve meaning and avoid duplicate records.

Alias merge should require evidence.

Safe merge signs:

* same effect
* same source
* same method
* same actor
* explicit “also called”
* repeated consistent use

Unsafe merge examples:

* Fireball and Flame Blade
* Shadow Step and Shadow Cloak
* Healing Light and Holy Ward
* Iron Skin and Stone Form

Do not merge abilities just because names are similar.

Do not rename a stable ability casually because AI output used a prettier phrase.

Tentative names can become aliases if the stable name changes.

⸻

19. Ability variants

A variant is a related form of an existing ability.

Examples:

* Mana Slash → Crescent Mana Slash
* Fireball → Compressed Fireball
* Shadow Step → Silent Shadow Step
* Healing Light → Cleansing Light
* Shield Bash → Guard Breaker

Variants should be linked to parent ability.

They should not always replace the parent.

Variant creation should require:

* stable parent ability
* clear new behavior
* repeated or explicit variation
* different cost, effect, target, or use case

Variant should not be created from every adjective.

Example:

“blue Mana Slash” does not necessarily create a new variant.

“Mana Slash splits into three crescent arcs at triple MP cost” may create a variant.

⸻

20. Ability evolution

Evolution is a major change in ability identity or power.

It should be rare and meaningful.

Evolution may occur from:

* mastery
* major story milestone
* divine blessing
* mutation
* artifact bond
* repeated high-stakes use
* teacher breakthrough
* ritual
* specialization threshold

Evolution should preserve:

* origin
* old name as alias if relevant
* parent link
* learned limits
* cost history
* previous rank or conversion notes

Evolution should not erase the ability’s meaning.

Example:

Mana Slash evolving into Crescent Mana Slash makes sense.

Mana Slash randomly becoming Time Stop does not.

⸻

21. Passive rules

Passives are dangerous because they can silently affect everything.

Passives need strict scope.

Every passive should define:

* trigger condition
* effect
* limits
* strength
* whether it is always-on or conditional
* whether it costs anything
* whether it can grow
* whether it stacks
* whether it appears in Context
* whether it is card-eligible

Examples:

Fire Resistance:

* trigger: fire exposure
* effect: reduce fire harm
* limit: not immunity, weaker against divine/magical flame unless confirmed
* growth: may improve through trait progression or story events

Battle Instinct:

* trigger: combat danger
* effect: improves reaction/recognition of threat
* limit: does not reveal invisible enemies automatically, does not replace SPD/INT

Mana Sensitivity:

* trigger: nearby magic
* effect: may detect magical presence
* limit: vague sense unless trained/refined

Passives should not stack infinitely.

Duplicate passives should merge or update.

⸻

22. Talent rules

Talents shape method.

A talent should define:

* domain
* style
* action family affected
* requirements
* costs if any
* strengths
* weaknesses
* growth path
* related stats
* related abilities
* Context behavior

Example:

Wolf Guard Stance:

* action family: defensive melee
* strengths: bracing, countering, holding ground
* cost: EP while sustained
* weakness: reduced mobility
* related stats: DEF, ATK
* related domain: Melee/Defense

Talents should not automatically create separate active skills unless the story supports it.

A talent can improve how normal actions are judged.

⸻

23. Specialization rules

Specialization describes long-term focus.

Specialization should be derived from repeated evidence.

Inputs:

* stable abilities
* talents
* passives
* domain ranks
* stat distribution
* repeated action history
* equipment preference
* quest behavior
* combat behavior
* social/crafting/investigation behavior

Outputs:

* specialization tags
* archetype summary
* build bias
* Context wording
* progression routing hints

Examples:

Repeated sword training + high ATK/SPD + dueling talent:

* specialization: duelist

High INT + Magic Domain + several spell skills:

* specialization: mage

Crafting Domain + recipes + item quality improvements:

* specialization: crafter / smith / alchemist depending context

Specialization should not be forced.

The player can be hybrid.

A specialization should not prevent off-build growth.

⸻

24. Build identity relationship

Build identity is not the same as ability ownership.

Build identity reads from:

* stats
* domains
* abilities
* talents
* passives
* equipment
* behavior
* resources
* repeated player choices

It produces:

* build tags
* archetype summary
* style hints

It should not directly create abilities.

Example:

If build identity says “agile duelist,” that does not grant a duelist skill.

It may make duelist-like training and ability stabilization more plausible.

⸻

25. Ability and progression relationship

Ability events may propose progression events.

Examples:

A real Mana Slash attempt may propose:

* Magic Domain XP
* Melee Domain XP
* INT XP
* ATK XP
* ability evidence

A successful shield talent use may propose:

* DEF XP
* Defense Domain XP
* talent evidence

A passive awakening may propose:

* relevant Domain XP
* maybe Level XP if major

But AbilityReducer does not directly write progression.

ProgressionReducer commits XP.

This follows the owner rule from Chunk 2.

⸻

26. Ability and resources relationship

Ability use may affect HP, MP, and EP.

Examples:

Mana Slash:

* MP cost
* small EP cost

Shield Bash:

* EP cost

Blood Magic:

* HP cost
* MP cost

Healing Light:

* MP cost
* possible fatigue

Shadow Step:

* MP and/or EP cost
* dizziness if failed

AbilityReducer should not directly write HP/MP/EP.

It should emit resource change events.

BodyReducer / PlayerReducer commits resources.

This preserves owner boundaries.

⸻

27. Ability and equipment relationship

Some abilities require equipment.

Examples:

Mana Slash may require:

* blade
* focus weapon
* mana edge substitute if rank high enough

Shield Bash requires:

* shield or shield-like object

Rune Strike requires:

* inscribed weapon
* rune focus

Bow technique requires:

* bow
* arrows or equivalent

If required equipment is unavailable, the ability may:

* fail
* become risky
* cost more
* produce weaker effect
* require improvised substitute

InventoryReducer owns equipment state.

AbilityReducer reads equipment or receives validation context.

It does not invent equipment.

⸻

28. Ability and Context behavior

Context should include abilities only when useful.

Context may include:

* most relevant stable abilities
* current unstable ability if actively being trained
* resource/cost warning
* key limits
* passive traits relevant to scene
* current talent/stance if active

Context should not dump:

* every candidate
* every failed attempt
* every passive
* every old ability note
* every cost observation

Good Context example:

“You know Mana Slash as a basic but costly sword-mana skill. It usually costs moderate MP and some EP, and is unreliable while exhausted.”

Bad Context example:

“Mana Slash evidence count 7, failed attempt id 12, cost confidence 0.42, aliases [blue edge, mana edge], last use turn 912.”

That belongs in audit/ability commands, not normal Context.

⸻

29. Ability and Story Card behavior

Generated ability cards should only be created for stable/card-eligible abilities.

Do not create generated cards for:

* hints
* weak candidates
* failed attempts
* pretend abilities
* jokes
* unstable one-off effects
* duplicate aliases
* passive noise
* generic traits

Ability cards may be appropriate for:

* signature stable skills
* important passives
* major talents
* evolved abilities
* story-defining powers
* abilities the AI must remember long-term

Ability cards should update when confirmed facts change:

* cost becomes known
* limit discovered
* ability rank increases
* alias confirmed
* variant appears
* passive strengthens
* ability evolves

CardSync mirrors AbilityReducer state.

CardSync does not create ability canon.

The clean-core CardSync rules allow stabilized/card-eligible ability cards but forbid CardSync from creating canon from raw prose.  ￼

31. Ability validation outcomes

Ability events use the same five validation outcomes.

Accept

Use when an ability event is confirmed enough.

Examples:

* real successful use
* confirmed breakthrough
* teacher-confirmed learning
* stabilized ability activation
* observed cost from clear use

Reject

Use when a proposed ability event is unsupported.

Examples:

* output mentions ability name but no use
* player intended ability but result did not occur
* vague “you feel power” with no action
* ability claim conflicts with state and lacks support

Pending

Use when ability possibility exists but is not active truth.

Examples:

* teacher says player may learn later
* book describes a spell
* prophecy mentions dormant power
* item may grant ability when bonded

Soft_fail

Use when real attempt fails or partially fails.

Examples:

* spell fizzles
* stance collapses
* shadow step moves only inches
* healing light reduces pain but not wound

Hard_deny

Use when ability event is non-canon or unsafe.

Examples:

* pretend
* joke
* quote
* dream treated as real
* rumor
* debug/card text
* impossible unsupported mastery
* explicit failure parsed as success

32. Ability safety rules

32.1 No ability from pretend

Pretend use creates no candidate.

32.2 No stable ability from failure alone

Failure may create evidence, not stable success.

32.3 No generated card for candidates

Only stable/card-eligible abilities can get cards.

32.4 No costless active skills by default

Active skills usually require MP, EP, time, tools, materials, risk, or opportunity.

32.5 No infinite passive stacking

Duplicate passives merge or update.

32.6 No random renaming

Stable ability names should not change from one output.

New names become aliases unless clearly evolved.

32.7 No ability rewriting from cards

Card text does not create or edit ability canon.

32.8 No mastery without evidence

High rank requires sustained use, training, milestone, or major story support.

32.9 No ability ignoring resources

Low MP/EP, injuries, conditions, equipment loss, and counters still matter.

32.10 No ability solving everything

Abilities have scope and limits.

Mana Slash succeeds repeatedly or receives clear breakthrough confirmation.

* CardSync may mirror if card-eligible

Ability is used several times with resource narration.

* cost estimate improves
* cost confidence increases
* ability summary updates

Player has insufficient MP/EP.

* no free full effect

Player has Fire Resistance.

35. Final Chunk 6 doctrine

Abilities grow through evidence.

Skills are active techniques.

Talents shape methods.

Passives are always-on or conditional capabilities.

Traits describe persistent qualities.

Specializations describe long-term focus.

Candidates are not stable abilities.

Failure can teach.

Pretending does not teach.

Costs become known through use.

Abilities improve through practice, pressure, teachers, breakthroughs, and story support.

Every ability has limits.

Every active ability has some cost, risk, condition, or opportunity cost unless clearly defined otherwise.

AbilityReducer owns ability truth.

ProgressionReducer owns XP.

BodyReducer owns resources and injuries.

InventoryReducer owns equipment.

CardSync mirrors only stable/card-eligible abilities.

Context reports only what is relevant.

The goal is not to collect random powers.

The goal is to let the player’s capabilities emerge, stabilize, and evolve naturally through the story.
