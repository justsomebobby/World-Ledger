AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 12: Investigation, Clues, Evidence, Witnesses, and Reputation

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 12 defines how AIDRPG handles truth, suspicion, proof, rumors, witnesses, social memory, public knowledge, reputation, titles, and solved cases.

This chunk answers:

What is an investigation?

What is a case?

What is a clue?

What is evidence?

What is the difference between a lead, clue, evidence, proof, rumor, and witness knowledge?

When is a case solved?

How does the system avoid treating rumors as truth?

How does reputation change?

How do private witnesses differ from public reputation?

How do titles work?

How does AIDRPG prevent false accusations, false case completion, and reputation drift?

The central rule is:

Investigation and reputation must separate what happened, what is believed, what is known, what is proven, and who knows it.

A rumor is not proof.

A clue is not proof.

A witness claim is not always truth.

A confession may be evidence, but only if directly validated.

Public reputation only changes when knowledge becomes public or socially recognized.

⸻

2. Core philosophy

Investigation should make mystery gameplay feel coherent.

The player should be able to:

* notice clues
* gather evidence
* interview witnesses
* track suspects
* expose contradictions
* solve cases
* wrongly suspect someone
* prove or disprove claims
* preserve evidence items
* affect reputation through public discoveries
* gain titles or legal consequences
* archive solved cases

But the system must avoid dangerous shortcuts.

It should not:

* solve a case from one vague clue
* treat rumor as truth
* treat suspicion as evidence
* make every NPC statement true
* make every clue public knowledge
* change reputation from private information
* create titles from casual praise
* convict suspects without proof
* create evidence cards for every minor detail
* complete quests from unverified claims

Investigation should support uncertainty.

That means the system needs levels of confidence instead of only true/false.

⸻

3. Ownership boundaries

Investigation and reputation are related, but they are not the same system.

InvestigationReducer owns

* cases
* leads
* clues
* evidence
* suspects
* contradictions
* witness statements as case material
* evidence confidence
* case confidence
* solved/unsolved state
* solved-case archive
* case links to actors, items, places, quests, contracts, and reputation

ReputationReducer owns

* public reputation
* local reputation
* faction reputation
* rumors
* private witness knowledge
* pending reports
* public deeds
* titles
* legal/social status
* known accusations
* social consequences

ActorReducer owns

* actor identity
* actor relationships
* actor knowledge if tied to specific actor memory
* suspect actor links
* witness actor links
* culprit actor links if confirmed

InventoryReducer owns

* evidence items as physical items
* custody of evidence
* ownership/access of evidence
* item condition

QuestReducer owns

* investigation-related quest objectives
* rewards
* quest completion

TimeReducer owns

* deadlines
* time-sensitive cases
* witness availability over time
* execution/trial deadlines
* rumor spread over time if used

CardSync owns

* case cards
* generated important evidence/item cards
* reputation summary cards
* actor cards updated from confirmed investigation/reputation state

The rule:

Investigation decides what is known or proven inside a case.

Reputation decides what people believe or say publicly.

Inventory owns evidence objects.

Quest owns quest objective state.

4. Core distinction: truth versus knowledge

AIDRPG must separate four things.

Actual canon truth

What really happened in state, if known.

Example:

Mara stole the ring.

This is only canon if validated by strong events or scenario setup.

Case knowledge

What the player or case file has discovered.

Example:

A silver thread matching Mara’s cloak was found.

This may point toward Mara, but does not prove she stole the ring alone.

Witness knowledge

What a specific actor saw, heard, believes, or claims.

Example:

Harlan says he saw Mara near the window.

This is Harlan’s statement. It may be true, mistaken, or false.

Public reputation

What the public, a local area, or faction believes about the player or someone else.

Example:

The village believes the player exposed the thief.

This affects social standing whether or not everyone knows the full truth.

These layers must not collapse into each other.

5. Investigation terms

5.1 Lead

A lead is a possible direction to investigate.

Examples:

* Mara was seen near the window
* the old chapel may be connected
* someone bought poison yesterday
* muddy footprints lead north
* a rumor says the mayor is hiding something

Leads are not evidence.

Leads point the player somewhere.

A lead can become a clue or evidence after validation.

5.2 Clue

A clue is an observed fact that may matter.

Examples:

* muddy footprints under the window
* torn blue cloth on a nail
* faint smell of bitter almond
* broken lock
* ash on the windowsill
* missing ledger page
* strange rune carved into doorframe

Clues are stronger than leads but may still be ambiguous.

A clue says:

Something relevant was found.

It does not necessarily prove who did it.

5.3 Evidence

Evidence is a clue, item, statement, or fact that supports or disproves a claim.

Examples:

* bloodstained dagger matching the wound
* signed confession
* poison vial with merchant mark
* footprint matching suspect’s boots
* witness testimony placing suspect at scene
* ledger showing payment
* recovered stolen ring in suspect’s bag

Evidence should have a confidence level.

Evidence can be weak, moderate, strong, confirmed, or disproven.

5.4 Proof

Proof is evidence strong enough to support a conclusion.

Proof may require one major confirmed piece or several supporting pieces.

Example:

Mara confesses directly while holding the stolen ring.

Example:

The stolen ring is found in Harlan’s locked chest, and Harlan’s boot matches the muddy print, and a witness saw him enter the room.

Proof depends on case rules and story context.

5.5 Suspect

A suspect is an actor who might be responsible.

A suspect is not guilty by default.

Suspect states:

* possible
* likely
* unlikely
* cleared
* accused
* confirmed culprit
* false accusation
* unknown

5.6 Culprit

A culprit is the confirmed responsible actor or force.

A culprit should only be assigned with strong evidence or direct validated truth.

Do not set culprit from rumor alone.

5.7 Contradiction

A contradiction is evidence or statement that conflicts with another claim.

Examples:

* Harlan says he was at the tavern, but the tavern was closed
* Mara claims she never entered the room, but her cloak thread is found there
* the ring was stolen at midnight, but suspect was seen elsewhere
* poison killed the victim, but the suspect only had a blunt weapon

Contradictions are valuable case progress.

They can weaken suspects, expose lies, or create new leads.

5.8 Case

A case is the structured investigation container.

Examples:

* missing ring case
* poisoned merchant case
* haunted mine disappearances
* guild spy case
* arson at the chapel
* murder at Greyford Inn

A case organizes leads, clues, evidence, suspects, witnesses, contradictions, and solution state.

7. Case lifecycle

7.1 Possible case

A possible case exists when something seems suspicious but no formal investigation has begun.

Example:

“Mara’s ring is missing.”

This may create a possible case or quest hook.

7.2 Case opened

A case opens when the player starts investigating or the story clearly frames it as an investigation.

Example:

“You decide to investigate who stole Mara’s ring.”

Status:

* open

7.3 Leads gathered

The player finds directions to investigate.

Example:

“Harlan says he saw someone near the window.”

Lead added.

7.4 Clues discovered

The player finds observed facts.

Example:

“You find muddy footprints below the window.”

Clue added.

7.5 Evidence established

A clue becomes evidence when it supports or disproves a claim.

Example:

“The muddy footprint matches Harlan’s boots.”

Evidence added.

7.6 Suspects tracked

Actors become possible suspects.

Example:

Mara, Harlan, and the stranger become suspects for different reasons.

Suspect status should be evidence-based.

7.7 Contradictions found

Conflicting statements or evidence are recorded.

Example:

Harlan said he never left the tavern, but the tavern keeper says he left before midnight.

7.8 Theory formed

The player or system may summarize a possible theory.

Theories are not canon.

Example:

“Harlan may have planted the thread to frame Mara.”

Theory should be marked as theory, not fact.

7.9 Ready to conclude

The case has enough strong evidence to support a conclusion.

This does not necessarily mean the conclusion has been reported or accepted publicly.

7.10 Solved

A case is solved when the responsible truth is confirmed enough.

This may require:

* direct confession
* direct witnessed act
* strong evidence chain
* recovered item with clear link
* contradiction resolved
* culprit identified
* objective met

7.11 Reported / publicly resolved

A solved case may be reported to an authority, NPC, faction, or public.

This is when reputation or quest completion may trigger.

7.12 Archived

Solved, failed, abandoned, or outdated cases move to archive.

Archive preserves summary and consequences.

⸻

8. Evidence confidence levels

Evidence should have confidence levels.

8.1 Unverified

The claim exists but has not been checked.

Example:

“Someone says Harlan was near the window.”

8.2 Weak

The clue suggests something but is ambiguous.

Example:

“Muddy footprints lead away from the room.”

Could belong to many people.

8.3 Moderate

The evidence supports a claim but does not prove it alone.

Example:

“The footprints are the same size as Harlan’s boots.”

8.4 Strong

The evidence strongly supports a claim.

Example:

“The boot print matches the distinctive broken heel of Harlan’s boot.”

8.5 Confirmed

The fact is directly validated.

Examples:

* direct confession
* witnessed act
* magical truth verification if reliable
* item found in suspect’s possession with strong link
* multiple independent strong evidence pieces

8.6 Disproven

The evidence or claim is shown false.

Example:

The footprint thought to be Harlan’s is proven to belong to a guard.

Evidence should be able to move up or down as new facts appear.

⸻

9. Evidence types

Physical evidence

Objects or traces.

Examples:

* weapon
* blood
* footprints
* fibers
* broken lock
* poison vial
* letter
* ledger
* stolen item

InventoryReducer owns the item.

InvestigationReducer owns its evidence meaning.

Testimonial evidence

Statements from witnesses, suspects, victims, or authorities.

Examples:

* witness saw suspect
* suspect confesses
* guard heard scream
* shopkeeper sold poison

Actor/Reputation may track who knows or claims it.

InvestigationReducer stores statement as case evidence.

Circumstantial evidence

Facts that suggest but do not prove.

Examples:

* suspect had motive
* suspect was nearby
* suspect owns similar weapon
* suspect lied about location

Useful but rarely enough alone.

Direct evidence

Evidence that directly supports conclusion.

Examples:

* seeing culprit commit act
* direct confession
* stolen item found in culprit’s locked chest
* magical recording if setting supports it

Contradictory evidence

Evidence that disproves, weakens, or complicates a claim.

Examples:

* alibi
* impossible timeline
* forged letter
* planted evidence
* wrong weapon type

Magical evidence

Magical investigation results.

Examples:

* truth spell
* memory echo
* spirit witness
* aura trace
* scrying vision
* divination answer

Magical evidence needs reliability rules.

It can be:

* confirmed if magic is trusted and validated
* strong if likely but fallible
* weak if dreamlike or symbolic
* dangerous if curse/illusion may interfere

Do not automatically treat every vision as truth.

⸻

10. Clue versus evidence decision table

Use this distinction:

If it only suggests a direction, it is a lead.

If it is observed and may matter, it is a clue.

If it supports or disproves a claim, it is evidence.

If it is strong enough to justify a conclusion, it may be proof.

Examples:

“A rumor says Harlan did it.”

* rumor / lead

“Muddy footprints are beneath the window.”

* clue

“The footprints match Harlan’s broken-heel boots.”

* evidence

“Harlan confesses he climbed through the window and stole the ring.”

* confirmed evidence / possible proof

“The ring is found in Harlan’s locked chest, and the footprint matches his boots.”

* strong evidence chain

⸻

11. Witness knowledge

Witness knowledge is what a specific actor knows, saw, heard, believes, or claims.

Witness knowledge should track:

* witness actor
* claim
* source event
* confidence
* whether firsthand or hearsay
* whether private or public
* whether truthful, mistaken, lying, or unknown if discoverable
* linked case
* linked reputation
* whether reported to authority
* whether contradicted
* last updated turn

Witness knowledge is not automatically public.

Example:

Mara privately tells the player she saw Harlan near the window.

That is:

* witness knowledge
* possibly testimonial lead/evidence
* not public reputation unless shared or overheard

Witness statements may be:

* true
* false
* mistaken
* incomplete
* coerced
* magically influenced
* rumor-based
* self-serving

The system should not assume witness statements are true unless validation says they are.

⸻

12. Rumors

Rumors are claims circulating socially.

Rumors may be true, false, exaggerated, or unknown.

Rumor records should track:

* rumor text
* source if known
* subject
* target actor/place/faction/player
* location/faction where rumor spreads
* confidence as rumor, not truth
* truth status if known separately
* spread level
* harm/benefit
* linked case/quest/reputation
* last updated turn

Rumor truth levels:

* unverified
* likely false
* possible
* likely true
* confirmed true
* disproven
* intentionally planted

Important:

A rumor can be accepted as a rumor while rejected as physical truth.

Example:

“A rumor spreads that you died in the woods.”

Accepted:

* reputation.rumor.add

Rejected:

* body.death

This distinction is mandatory.

⸻

13. Public versus private knowledge

AIDRPG must separate private facts from public reputation.

Private knowledge

Known only to player, companion, witness, suspect, or small group.

Examples:

* Mara privately confessed
* player found hidden letter
* witness saw culprit but has not told anyone
* player knows ring is fake
* companion knows player lied

Private knowledge can affect:

* dialogue
* investigation
* actor relationship
* blackmail
* future choices

It should not automatically change public reputation.

Public knowledge

Known broadly enough to affect social standing.

Examples:

* crowd saw player save child
* town guard announced culprit
* player publicly exposed thief
* guild officially grants title
* rumor spreads through village
* trial verdict announced

Public knowledge can affect:

* reputation
* titles
* faction standing
* prices
* NPC reactions
* legal status
* quests

Rule:

Private discovery does not equal public fame.

14. Reputation model

Reputation is what people, places, or factions believe about the player or another actor.

Reputation should track:

* subject
* reputation scope
* reputation type
* value or tags
* source
* confidence/publicity
* witnesses
* linked deeds
* linked rumors
* linked titles
* current standing
* last updated turn

Reputation scopes:

* personal
* local
* settlement
* faction
* regional
* global
* underworld
* legal
* religious
* guild
* party/internal

Reputation types:

* heroic
* trustworthy
* dangerous
* criminal
* honorable
* dishonorable
* merciful
* cruel
* skilled
* cowardly
* cursed
* noble
* suspicious
* famous
* infamous
* unreliable

Reputation should usually be tag-based and scoped rather than one universal number.

Example:

The player may be respected by villagers but wanted by the city guard.

⸻

15. Reputation change rules

Reputation can change when:

* deed is witnessed
* deed is reported
* rumor spreads
* authority declares result
* faction records action
* public quest is completed
* contract is fulfilled or breached
* case is solved publicly
* title is granted
* crime is discovered
* mercy/cruelty is observed
* public duel is won
* domain project benefits community

Reputation should not change from:

* private action nobody knows about
* player intent alone
* dream
* rumor being treated as fact
* unverified claim unless it is stored as rumor
* card text
* debug text
* one random compliment unless socially meaningful

Reputation can change from rumor even if rumor is false, but it should be marked as rumor-based.

Example:

False rumor:

“The town believes you stole the ring.”

Reputation may suffer locally even if player did not do it.

But the actual case truth should remain separate.

⸻

16. Reputation values

The system can use tags, scores, or both.

Recommended model:

Use scoped tags plus light numeric values.

Example:

Local reputation in Greyford:

* helpful +2
* suspicious -1
* wolf-slayer +1
* oathbreaker -3

Faction reputation with Silver Guild:

* trusted +3
* debtor -1

Legal status:

* wanted
* cleared
* under suspicion
* pardoned
* outlaw

This allows nuance.

One global reputation score is too simplistic.

17. Titles

Titles are socially recognized labels.

Examples:

* Wolf-Slayer
* Ring-Finder
* Friend of Greyford
* Oathbreaker
* Guild Apprentice
* Knight of Northwatch
* Wanted Thief
* Blessed of the Moon
* Cursebearer
* Champion of the Wall
* Exile

A title should require recognition.

Titles can come from:

* authority grant
* public deed
* faction ceremony
* repeated reputation
* legal ruling
* divine/magical mark
* public rumor if title is informal
* domain status

Titles should not come from:

* one private compliment
* player claiming it
* dream
* joke
* card text
* weak flavor

Title records should track:

* title name
* subject
* granting source
* scope
* public/private
* benefits
* drawbacks
* linked deed
* status
* revocation condition if any

Titles can affect Context and social interactions.

But titles should be rare enough to matter.

18. Legal and social status

Legal/social status is a structured reputation consequence.

Examples:

* wanted
* accused
* cleared
* pardoned
* exiled
* outlaw
* under investigation
* protected witness
* guild member
* noble guest
* banned from town
* debtor
* oathbound

Legal status should require authority, public accusation, law/faction action, or clear social recognition.

Example:

A guard captain declares the player wanted for theft.

Legal status may update.

Example:

A random drunk calls the player a thief.

This is insult or rumor, not legal status.

Legal status can link to:

* reputation
* quests
* contracts
* actors
* factions
* places
* travel restrictions
* prices
* guards
* deadlines

19. Solving cases

A case is solved when the central question is answered with sufficient validated evidence.

Case solution should include:

* conclusion
* culprit or cause
* evidence supporting conclusion
* unresolved doubts if any
* who knows the solution
* whether it was reported
* consequences
* archive summary

Possible solved states:

Privately solved

The player knows the answer, but has not reported it.

Example:

The player discovers Harlan stole the ring but has not confronted anyone.

Publicly solved

The answer is revealed or accepted by relevant public/authority.

Example:

The guard accepts the evidence and arrests Harlan.

Officially solved

Authority/faction/legal system recognizes solution.

Example:

The mayor declares the case closed.

Falsely solved

A conclusion was accepted but later evidence may show it is wrong.

Example:

Mara is blamed based on planted evidence.

Unsolved but closed

Case abandoned, failed, or closed without truth.

This may happen if evidence is lost, deadline passes, or authority refuses.

⸻

20. Solved-case archive

Solved or closed cases should archive.

Archive should track:

* case title
* result
* culprit/cause if known
* major evidence
* false leads
* witnesses
* public/private resolution
* reputation effects
* quest links
* rewards
* unresolved notes
* consequences
* date/time if available

Archive prevents clutter while preserving history.

Archived cases may later matter if:

* culprit returns
* false solution exposed
* reputation questioned
* old evidence becomes relevant
* title/reward depends on case
* faction remembers outcome

⸻

21. Investigation and quests

Investigation can be linked to quests.

Example:

Quest:

“Find Mara’s missing ring.”

Investigation case:

“Who stole Mara’s ring?”

The quest may complete when the ring is returned.

The case may solve when culprit is identified.

Those are related but not identical.

Possible outcomes:

* ring found, culprit unknown
* culprit identified, ring still missing
* wrong person accused
* ring returned quietly, no public case
* culprit exposed publicly, reputation changes
* case solved but quest reward pending

The system must not complete both automatically unless both conditions are met.

22. Investigation and inventory

Evidence items are physical items plus evidence meaning.

Example:

Bloodstained dagger.

InventoryReducer owns:

* dagger item
* custody
* condition
* location
* ownership

InvestigationReducer owns:

* dagger is evidence in murder case
* blood matches victim
* found in Harlan’s room
* confidence level

If the dagger is lost:

Inventory updates custody/access.

Investigation may mark evidence unavailable or compromised.

If the dagger is cleaned:

Inventory updates condition.

Investigation may reduce evidence strength.

23. Investigation and actors

Actors can be:

* victim
* witness
* suspect
* culprit
* investigator
* authority
* informant
* accuser
* accused
* accomplice
* alibi source

ActorReducer owns who they are.

InvestigationReducer owns their case role.

ReputationReducer owns what people believe about them.

Example:

Mara is an actor.

Mara is suspect in missing ring case.

Mara is publicly accused in Greyford.

These are related but separate truths.

24. Investigation and reputation

Investigation can affect reputation when knowledge spreads.

Examples:

Player privately finds culprit:

* no public reputation yet

Player publicly exposes culprit:

* reputation increases locally
* suspect legal status changes
* quest may complete
* title may be granted

Player falsely accuses innocent person:

* reputation may suffer
* actor relationship changes
* legal/social risk
* case remains unresolved or falsely solved

Player hides evidence:

* private actor/reputation consequences if discovered
* possible legal risk later

Public knowledge is the bridge from investigation to reputation.

⸻

25. Rumor spread

Rumors may spread over time or by events.

Rumor spread depends on:

* number of witnesses
* public location
* social importance
* faction interest
* scandal level
* player fame
* actor importance
* time passing
* deliberate spreading
* suppression efforts
* evidence supporting or disproving rumor

Rumor may affect:

* local reputation
* faction reputation
* prices
* NPC behavior
* guard attention
* quest opportunities
* actor relationships

Rumor should not affect:

* physical state
* inventory
* HP
* confirmed case truth
* actual guilt
* actual death

unless later validated separately.

⸻

26. Witness publicity levels

Witness knowledge should have publicity levels.

Private

Only witness and maybe player know.

Shared

Small group knows.

Reported

Authority, faction, or relevant NPC has been told.

Public

Community broadly knows.

Widespread

Regional or faction-wide.

Legendary

Long-term/public title-level knowledge.

Reputation changes should depend on publicity.

A private confession does not create public fame until revealed.

⸻

27. Investigation Context behavior

Context should include investigation facts only when relevant.

Good Context examples:

“You are investigating Mara’s missing ring. Current clues: muddy footprints under the window, a silver thread caught on the frame, and Harlan’s inconsistent alibi.”

“Mara is only a suspect, not confirmed guilty. The silver thread points toward her cloak, but the footprint evidence is unresolved.”

“You privately know Harlan confessed, but the village does not know yet.”

Bad Context examples:

Dumping every clue from every old case every turn.

Telling the AI “Harlan is guilty” when the case only has weak evidence.

Turning a rumor into confirmed truth.

Listing hidden culprit if the player has not discovered it, unless state/story setup explicitly allows the AI to know.

Context should be careful with uncertainty.

Use wording like:

* suspected
* rumored
* claimed
* witnessed
* confirmed
* disproven
* privately known
* publicly known

⸻

28. Reputation Context behavior

Context should include reputation when it affects the scene.

Examples:

“You are known in Greyford as the person who exposed the ring thief.”

“The guards are suspicious of you because a rumor claims you stole from the chapel.”

“The Silver Guild trusts you after you fulfilled their contract.”

“Mara privately trusts you, but the village still suspects her.”

Do not inject every reputation tag every turn.

Reputation Context should be scoped.

If the player is in Greyford, local Greyford reputation matters.

If the player meets Silver Guild members, Silver Guild reputation matters.

If the player is alone in a forest, public reputation may not matter unless pursuers or rumors are relevant.

29. Investigation and reputation Story Card behavior

Cards should be selective.

Generated or protected cards may mirror:

* major active case
* solved case archive summary
* important evidence item
* major public rumor
* important reputation status
* title
* legal status
* faction reputation
* important witness/culprit actor update

Do not create cards for:

* every clue
* every minor witness statement
* every rumor
* every suspicion
* every small reputation shift
* every case note
* every possible suspect

Protected cards may include:

* Investigation & Evidence Summary
* Reputation & Titles Summary
* Quest Log
* Actor cards for major suspects/witnesses/culprits
* Item cards for important evidence items

CardSync mirrors state.

Cards do not create case truth or reputation truth.

30. Investigation validation outcomes

Investigation uses the same five outcomes.

Accept

Use when a clue, evidence, statement, case update, or reputation change is directly supported.

Examples:

* player finds footprint
* witness directly states claim
* suspect confesses
* evidence item found
* public crowd witnesses deed
* authority grants title

Reject

Use when proposed investigation/reputation change is unsupported.

Examples:

* rumor treated as proof
* clue not actually found
* suspect only imagined
* public reputation from private event
* title from casual compliment
* case completion from weak clue

Pending

Use when information may matter but is unverified.

Examples:

* possible suspect
* rumor
* lead
* promised witness meeting
* possible evidence location
* unverified claim

Soft_fail

Use when real investigation action produces partial result.

Examples:

* search finds clue but not proof
* witness refuses to talk but reveals nervousness
* evidence is damaged but partially readable
* accusation fails but causes reaction
* interrogation reveals contradiction but not confession

Hard_deny

Use when event is non-canon or unsafe.

Examples:

* dream clue treated as physical fact
* quote treated as confession
* card text creates solved case
* debug text creates reputation
* pretend investigation
* impossible instant solution without evidence

⸻

31. Investigation safety rules

31.1 No rumor as proof

Rumors may become rumor records, not evidence proof.

31.2 No clue as conclusion

One clue rarely solves a case by itself.

31.3 No public reputation from private knowledge

Someone must witness, hear, report, or spread it.

31.4 No confession from quoted example

A quoted or hypothetical confession is not real confession.

31.5 No title from casual praise

Titles require recognition.

31.6 No case completion without solution condition

Finding one clue is progress, not completion.

31.7 No false culprit without confidence tracking

Suspects need status and evidence confidence.

31.8 No inventory evidence without item ownership/custody

Evidence item must exist if physical.

31.9 No duplicate evidence/reputation

Repeated output does not duplicate clues, evidence, rumors, titles, or reputation gain.

31.10 No card-created case truth

Cards mirror investigation state; they do not solve cases.

31.11 No hidden truth forced into player Context

If player has not discovered culprit, Context should not reveal it unless the design explicitly allows omniscient AI guidance.

31.12 No universal reputation

Reputation must be scoped.

32. Example scenarios

Example 1 — Rumor as lead

Output:

“People whisper that Harlan stole Mara’s ring.”

Expected:

* reputation.rumor.add or investigation.lead.add

35. Final Chunk 12 doctrine

Investigation is about discovering truth.

Reputation is about what people believe, know, say, and recognize.

A lead points somewhere.

A clue suggests something.

Evidence supports or disproves a claim.

Proof justifies a conclusion.

A suspect is not a culprit.

A witness statement is not automatically true.

A rumor is not proof.

Private knowledge is not public reputation.

A case can be privately solved before it is publicly resolved.

Titles require recognition.

Legal status requires authority or social/legal consequence.

Physical evidence belongs to Inventory.

Evidence meaning belongs to Investigation.

Public belief belongs to Reputation.

Actors own identity.

Quests own objectives.

Cards mirror stable important investigation/reputation state.

Context must preserve uncertainty instead of overstating it.

The goal is to let mysteries, accusations, witnesses, rumors, titles, and reputation feel alive without corrupting canon truth.
