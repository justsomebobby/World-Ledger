AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 11: Quests, Contracts, Labor, Domain Projects, and Objectives

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 11 defines how AIDRPG handles objectives.

This chunk answers:

What is a quest?

What is a contract?

What is labor?

What is a worker?

What is a foreman?

What is a domain project?

How does the system decide whether something is a quest, contract, investigation, crafting job, labor order, or domain project?

How do objectives start, progress, complete, fail, archive, and reward the player?

How do deadlines work?

How does AIDRPG avoid turning every task into five overlapping systems?

The central rule is:

Objectives must have a clear primary type.

A single story situation can link multiple systems, but each objective should have one main owner.

⸻

2. Core philosophy

AIDRPG should help AI Dungeon remember what the player is trying to accomplish.

It should track:

* active quests
* accepted tasks
* quest objectives
* promised rewards
* deadlines
* contracts
* obligations
* worker assignments
* domain projects
* progress
* blockers
* completion
* failure
* archives

But it should not turn every casual request into a quest.

It should not create domain projects from every mention of a building.

It should not create contracts from every conversation.

It should not create labor systems from every helpful NPC.

It should not complete objectives from rumors, vague claims, or quoted dialogue.

The goal is objective clarity, not objective spam.

⸻

3. Ownership boundaries

Objectives touch many systems, but each type has a main owner.

QuestReducer owns

* quest offers
* accepted quests
* active quests
* quest objectives
* quest progress
* quest completion
* quest failure
* quest abandonment
* quest archive
* quest rewards as proposals
* quest links to actors, places, items, cases, contracts, and deadlines

ContractReducer owns

* explicit agreements
* parties
* terms
* payment obligations
* service obligations
* delivery obligations
* item custody obligations
* deadlines
* breach
* fulfillment
* cancellation
* contract archive

LaborReducer owns

* workers
* worker groups
* work orders
* assigned tasks
* foremen
* morale
* wages
* productivity
* injuries/casualties if relevant
* labor availability

DomainReducer owns

* bases
* holdings
* settlements
* workshops
* walls
* roads
* farms
* domain assets
* domain resources
* domain projects
* construction/repair progress
* project blockers
* project completion/archive

InvestigationReducer owns

* cases
* clues
* evidence
* suspects
* solved-case archive

A mystery objective may link to a quest, but the case evidence belongs to InvestigationReducer.

InventoryReducer owns

* quest items
* materials
* payment items
* custody items
* rewards once granted

EconomyReducer owns

* payments
* prices
* wages
* fees
* debt
* rewards involving currency

TimeReducer owns

* deadlines
* elapsed time
* project timing
* travel time
* recovery ticks

ProgressionReducer owns

* XP rewards
* domain XP
* stat XP
* level rewards
* anti-farm reward control

ReputationReducer owns

* public reputation
* faction reaction
* witness knowledge
* titles
* legal/social consequences

CardSync owns

* quest cards
* contract cards
* domain cards
* protected summaries
* only after objective state is stable and important

The rule is:

QuestReducer does not directly add items.

ContractReducer does not directly spend coins.

DomainReducer does not directly award XP.

Each owner proposes follow-up events for the correct owner.

4. Objective type decision table

This is one of the most important parts of the whole design.

When the story presents a task, AIDRPG must classify it correctly.

Quest

A quest is a personal or adventure objective.

Examples:

* find the missing ring
* rescue the trapped miner
* defeat the graveyard beast
* deliver the sealed letter
* escort Mara to the old chapel
* retrieve the stolen relic
* investigate the haunted tower
* reach Northwatch before sunset

Quest features:

* objective-focused
* story/adventure driven
* may have a giver
* may have reward
* may have deadline
* may link to actors, places, items, cases, contracts, reputation, or combat

Primary owner:

* QuestReducer

Contract

A contract is an agreement with terms between parties.

Examples:

* Mira repairs your sword for 5 silver by morning
* you agree to deliver a crate for payment
* you hire guards for 2 silver each
* you rent a horse until sunset
* the guild lends you 20 silver due next week
* a merchant agrees to buy wolf pelts at 1 silver each

Contract features:

* parties
* terms
* obligation
* payment or exchange
* fulfillment/breach
* deadline or condition
* custody or service often involved

Primary owner:

* ContractReducer

Labor

Labor is work performed by workers or worker groups.

Examples:

* five carpenters repair the wall
* workers haul timber
* miners clear a blocked tunnel
* apprentices brew potions under supervision
* guards patrol the road
* villagers dig a trench

Labor features:

* worker count
* role
* task
* morale
* productivity
* pay/contract link
* foreman or leader
* possible domain/project link

Primary owner:

* LaborReducer

Domain project

A domain project is a managed base, holding, settlement, infrastructure, or organization objective.

Examples:

* repair the north wall
* restore Northwatch Keep
* build a forge
* clear farmland
* improve the road
* establish a watchtower
* stock the domain warehouse
* train militia
* rebuild the bridge
* expand the village shrine

Domain project features:

* large or managed objective
* tied to holding/base/settlement/resource
* progresses over time
* may require labor, materials, money, contracts, and deadlines
* affects the world beyond one personal item

Primary owner:

* DomainReducer

Investigation case

An investigation case is a mystery, crime, secret, or evidence-driven problem.

Examples:

* determine who stole the ring
* prove Mara’s innocence
* identify the poisoner
* find what caused the fires
* solve the missing workers case
* uncover the spy in the guild

Case features:

* leads
* clues
* evidence
* suspects
* witness knowledge
* contradictions
* solved/archive state

Primary owner:

* InvestigationReducer

Crafting project

A crafting project is making, repairing, upgrading, or altering an item.

Examples:

* forge a dagger
* repair shield
* brew antidote
* enchant cloak
* make lockpicks

Primary owner:

* CraftingReducer for workflow
* InventoryReducer for final item truth

Travel objective

A travel objective is movement to a place.

Examples:

* reach the old chapel
* travel to Greyford
* cross the Blackwood Road
* escape the burning inn

Primary owner:

* TimeReducer for travel/time
* PlaceReducer for current location/place truth
* QuestReducer only if it is a quest objective

6. Quest model

A quest is a structured adventure objective.

A quest should track:

* quest ID
* title
* status
* giver
* accepted by whom
* objective list
* current objective
* optional deadline
* reward promise
* actual rewards granted
* linked actors
* linked places
* linked items
* linked cases
* linked contracts
* linked domain projects
* progress notes
* blockers
* failure conditions
* completion evidence
* archive summary
* source/evidence text
* last updated turn
* card eligibility

Quest statuses:

* offered
* pending acceptance
* active
* progressing
* blocked
* ready to complete
* completed
* failed
* abandoned
* archived

⸻

7. Quest lifecycle

7.1 Quest hook

A quest hook is a possible task.

Example:

“Mara says someone stole her ring.”

This may create:

* quest hook
* investigation lead
* actor memory

It does not automatically create an accepted quest.

7.2 Quest offer

A quest offer is when an NPC, situation, or story clearly presents an objective.

Example:

“Mara asks you to find her missing ring.”

Status:

* offered

Still not accepted unless the player agrees or the story assigns it clearly.

7.3 Quest acceptance

Quest acceptance requires:

* player agrees
* story clearly assigns the task
* player takes responsibility
* command/manual acceptance if used
* scenario setup grants quest

Example:

“You promise Mara you will find the ring.”

Status:

* active

7.4 Quest progress

Quest progress is partial movement toward completion.

Examples:

* found a clue
* reached a location
* recovered required item
* defeated a blocking enemy
* spoke to witness
* delivered first part
* discovered suspect
* opened locked path

Quest progress should be tied to validated events.

7.5 Quest blocked

A quest is blocked when progress cannot continue until condition changes.

Examples:

* missing key
* NPC absent
* road blocked
* evidence missing
* item stolen
* deadline pressure
* contract failed
* player lacks material
* enemy too strong

Blocked quests should appear in commands, not always in Context unless relevant.

7.6 Ready to complete

The quest objective appears fulfilled, but reward/turn-in may not have occurred.

Example:

The player found the ring but has not returned it.

Status:

* ready to complete or objective complete
* not fully completed

7.7 Quest completion

Quest completion requires validated fulfillment.

Examples:

* item delivered
* enemy defeated
* person rescued
* message delivered
* case solved and reported
* destination reached
* contract fulfilled if linked

Quest completion may emit:

* progression.xp.gain
* inventory.item.gain
* inventory.coins.gain
* reputation.public.change
* actor.relationship.update
* quest.archive
* card.dirty

QuestReducer does not directly write those other states.

7.8 Quest failure

Quest failure occurs when the objective cannot be completed or is explicitly failed.

Examples:

* target dies
* deadline missed
* item destroyed
* player refuses after accepting
* enemy escapes permanently
* contract breached
* NPC no longer trusts player
* place destroyed

Failure should require clear evidence.

Do not fail quests from vague narration.

7.9 Quest abandonment

Abandonment occurs when the player intentionally drops the quest or leaves it inactive long enough under explicit rules.

Abandonment should be player-driven or strongly story-confirmed.

Do not silently abandon quests because they were not mentioned for a few turns.

7.10 Quest archive

Completed, failed, or abandoned quests should move to archive.

Archive should preserve:

* result
* important choices
* rewards
* consequences
* linked actors/places/items
* date/time if available

Archived quests should not appear as active Context unless relevant.

⸻

8. Quest validation rules

Accept quest offer if

* NPC or story clearly presents objective
* objective is understandable
* source is direct narration/dialogue
* not dream/rumor unless quest is specifically about a rumor
* not debug/card text
* not duplicate

Accept quest acceptance if

* player agrees
* player promises
* player takes task
* story explicitly assigns task
* scenario setup starts with quest active
* command accepts offered quest

Reject quest acceptance if

* NPC only mentions problem
* player only thinks about helping
* offer is refused
* statement is quoted by someone else
* dream/vision only
* no clear objective

Accept quest progress if

* objective-related event is confirmed
* target item found/taken
* enemy defeated
* witness spoken to
* location reached
* clue/evidence discovered
* required action done

Reject quest progress if

* result is rumor
* result is only planned
* action failed
* item not actually acquired
* enemy not actually defeated
* wrong target
* duplicate output

Accept quest completion if

* final objective fulfilled
* required turn-in/report happens if needed
* completion is directly narrated
* quest state supports completion
* duplicate reward blocked

Reject quest completion if

* someone merely says it is done
* rumor says it is done
* player intends to finish
* partial objective completed
* required item not delivered
* wrong quest/objective
* duplicate completion

9. Contract model

A contract is an agreement with obligations.

A contract should track:

* contract ID
* parties
* status
* terms
* service or item involved
* payment
* payment status
* custody status
* deadline
* location
* penalties
* collateral
* linked quest
* linked item
* linked worker group
* linked domain project
* fulfillment conditions
* breach conditions
* source/evidence
* archive summary

Contract statuses:

* proposed
* offered
* agreed
* active
* pending payment
* pending custody
* in progress
* fulfilled
* breached
* cancelled
* failed
* archived

⸻

10. Contract lifecycle

10.1 Contract proposal

A possible agreement is discussed.

Example:

“Mira says she could repair the sword for 5 silver.”

Not active yet.

10.2 Contract offer

One side offers terms.

Example:

“I’ll repair it for 5 silver by dawn.”

Status:

* offered

10.3 Agreement

Both sides agree.

Example:

“You agree to Mira’s price.”

Status:

* agreed or active, depending whether service begins

10.4 Payment/custody setup

Contracts often require payment or item transfer.

Example:

Mira needs the sword.

Inventory custody changes only when the sword is actually handed over.

10.5 Active contract

Work or obligation is underway.

Example:

Mira has the sword and is repairing it.

10.6 Fulfillment

The terms are satisfied.

Example:

Mira returns the repaired sword.

Contract fulfilled.

10.7 Breach

A party violates terms.

Examples:

* Mira keeps the sword
* player refuses agreed payment
* hired guards abandon job
* courier loses package
* merchant delivers fake item

Breach may emit:

* reputation event
* quest event
* economy/debt event
* inventory custody event
* actor relationship event

10.8 Cancellation

The contract ends without breach if both sides cancel or terms allow cancellation.

10.9 Archive

Fulfilled, breached, cancelled, or failed contracts move to archive.

⸻

11. Contract versus quest

This distinction must be strict.

A quest is about achieving a story objective.

A contract is about fulfilling agreed terms.

Example:

“Mara asks you to find the missing ring.”

Quest.

“Mara agrees to pay you 5 silver if you return the ring.”

Contract link or reward promise.

If the agreement/payment terms matter, create a linked contract or reward obligation.

If the adventure objective matters most, the quest remains primary.

Example:

“Deliver this crate to Greyford by sunset for 3 silver.”

This can be both:

* Quest: deliver crate to Greyford
* Contract: delivery agreement for 3 silver by sunset

But the primary type should depend on gameplay framing.

If it is a paid job with terms, primary contract.

If it is a story mission with reward, primary quest.

12. Labor model

Labor is organized work by people or groups.

A labor record should track:

* worker group ID
* display name
* count
* worker type
* skill level
* morale
* loyalty
* wage
* contract link
* current task
* assigned location
* foreman
* tools/materials
* productivity
* status
* casualties/injuries if relevant
* progress contribution
* source/evidence
* last updated turn

Worker statuses:

* available
* hired
* assigned
* working
* idle
* blocked
* tired
* injured
* unpaid
* refusing
* absent
* completed task
* dismissed
* dead/disbanded

13. Workers

Workers are people or groups who perform labor.

Examples:

* carpenters
* masons
* miners
* guards
* scouts
* farmers
* cooks
* smiths
* alchemists
* apprentices
* porters
* soldiers
* laborers
* healers
* scribes

Workers may be:

* hired
* assigned
* volunteering
* conscripted
* faction-provided
* contract-bound
* companion-led
* domain residents

Workers should usually be grouped.

Do not create individual actor records for every worker.

Promote an individual worker to actor only if named and important.

Example:

“Five carpenters” = worker group.

“Harlan, the foreman of the carpenters” = actor.

⸻

14. Foremen

A foreman is a named or role-based leader responsible for a worker group.

Foremen affect:

* productivity
* morale
* communication
* discipline
* task quality
* safety
* worker loyalty
* progress reporting
* conflict resolution

Foreman record may link to:

* ActorReducer if named
* LaborReducer as group leader
* DomainReducer if tied to project
* ContractReducer if hired

Example:

Harlan leads five carpenters repairing the wall.

Harlan is an actor if named and recurring.

The carpenters are a worker group.

The wall repair is a domain project.

The wage agreement is a contract/economy record.

⸻

15. Labor lifecycle

15.1 Labor need identified

Example:

“The wall needs repair.”

This may create domain project need.

No workers yet.

15.2 Workers found

Example:

“You find five carpenters willing to work.”

Creates possible worker group.

May be pending until hired/assigned.

15.3 Workers hired or assigned

Requires:

* agreement
* authority
* payment or obligation
* task clarity

Creates labor group active/assigned.

15.4 Work begins

Workers start task.

Time, materials, tools, and morale matter.

15.5 Progress

Workers contribute progress over time.

Progress may be slowed by:

* missing materials
* weather
* danger
* low morale
* poor tools
* no foreman
* lack of pay
* injury
* attack
* bad orders

15.6 Labor blocked

Work cannot continue.

Examples:

* no timber
* rain ruins mortar
* workers unpaid
* monster attacks
* foreman injured
* tools broken

15.7 Task complete

Labor objective finished.

May update domain project, contract, economy, reputation, or inventory.

15.8 Workers dismissed / reassigned

Worker group remains, leaves, or takes new task depending state.

18. Domain versus quest

This distinction matters.

A quest is about the player doing an adventure objective.

A domain project is about managing an asset or operation.

Example:

“Kill the spiders in the mine.”

Quest.

“Reopen the mine and restore production.”

Domain project.

The quest may be a step in the domain project.

Example relationship:

Domain project: Reopen Greyrock Mine.

Linked quest: Clear spiders from lower tunnel.

Linked labor: miners repair supports.

Linked contract: hire guards.

Linked economy: pay wages.

Linked inventory: timber supports.

This is how systems speak to each other without merging.

⸻

19. Domain versus labor

Labor is the workers and work effort.

Domain project is the managed objective.

Example:

Domain project:

* repair north wall

Labor:

* five masons assigned
* two carpenters assigned
* Harlan foreman

If workers leave, labor changes.

The project may remain blocked.

If project completes, labor can be reassigned.

⸻

20. Objective progress model

Progress can be tracked differently depending on objective type.

Quest progress

Use objective stages.

Example:

* speak to Mara
* search the room
* find clue
* recover ring
* return ring

Contract progress

Use terms.

Example:

* payment received
* item custody transferred
* repair completed
* item returned

Labor progress

Use work contribution over time.

Example:

* workers repaired 30% of wall

Domain project progress

Use percentage, stages, or milestones.

Example:

* materials gathered
* foundation cleared
* wall repaired 50%
* gate reinforced
* completed

Investigation progress

Use clue/evidence/case status.

Example:

* lead found
* suspect identified
* evidence confirmed
* culprit proven

The guide should avoid using one progress model for all objectives.

⸻

21. Objective blockers

Blockers are reasons progress cannot continue.

Common blockers:

* missing item
* missing material
* missing worker
* missing payment
* missing evidence
* locked location
* enemy threat
* injury/fatigue
* bad weather
* deadline pressure
* NPC absent
* route unavailable
* low reputation
* contract dispute
* insufficient authority
* unsafe conditions
* unresolved prerequisite quest

Blockers should be explicit and helpful.

Example:

Quest blocked:

“Need the rusted iron key to open the shrine door.”

Domain project blocked:

“Need 20 timber and at least 4 workers.”

Contract blocked:

“Mira cannot repair the sword until you deliver the mana crystal.”

Good blockers help the player know what to do.

Bad blockers silently stop progress without explanation.

⸻

22. Deadlines

Deadlines can attach to:

* quests
* contracts
* labor tasks
* domain projects
* travel objectives
* investigations
* reputation/legal events

TimeReducer owns deadline timing.

The objective owner owns what deadline means.

Deadline states:

* active
* approaching
* urgent
* missed
* extended
* completed
* cancelled

Examples:

Quest deadline:

* return before sunset

Contract deadline:

* deliver crate by dawn

Domain deadline:

* repair gate before raiders arrive

Investigation deadline:

* prove innocence before execution

Deadline effects:

* failure
* reduced reward
* reputation loss
* enemy advancement
* contract breach
* extra cost
* new quest branch

Deadlines should not be invented too freely.

They should be explicit or strongly implied.

⸻

23. Objective rewards

Rewards are proposed by objective owners but committed by proper systems.

Reward types:

* Level XP
* Domain XP
* Stat XP
* Ability evidence
* coins
* items
* reputation
* title
* actor relationship
* access/unlock
* contract fulfillment
* domain resource
* faction standing
* information
* route/place access

QuestReducer may propose rewards.

ProgressionReducer commits XP.

InventoryReducer commits items/coins.

ReputationReducer commits reputation/title.

ActorReducer commits relationship/memory.

PlaceReducer commits access/place changes.

DomainReducer commits domain resource changes.

Reward validation must check:

* objective completed
* reward promised or story-granted
* reward actually given if physical
* duplicate operation not applied
* correct recipient
* no weak rumor source

⸻

24. Objective reward timing

Rewards may be immediate or delayed.

Immediate reward

Example:

“Mara gives you 5 silver.”

Commit coins if accepted.

Promised reward

Example:

“Mara promises to pay you tomorrow.”

Create pending reward or contract obligation.

Do not add coins yet.

Conditional reward

Example:

“The guild will pay if you bring proof.”

Reward remains pending until proof delivered.

Reputation reward

Example:

“The crowd cheers your rescue.”

Reputation may change immediately if public and witnessed.

Hidden reward

Example:

“The old man silently marks you as trustworthy.”

May update actor relationship or hidden reputation, not public fame.

⸻

25. Objective failure consequences

Failure can create consequences.

Possible failure consequences:

* no reward
* reduced reward
* reputation loss
* actor trust loss
* contract breach
* debt
* item loss
* deadline missed
* quest branch changes
* enemy strengthened
* domain damage
* worker morale loss
* legal trouble
* case suspect escapes
* companion relationship damage

Failure should be validated.

Do not fail major objectives from vague narration.

Example:

“You worry you may be too late.”

Not failure.

“The sun sets before you reach the gate, and the caravan leaves without you.”

Deadline missed if deadline existed.

⸻

26. Objective archive

Archived objectives should preserve history.

Archive records should include:

* title
* type
* result
* major choices
* rewards
* failures
* linked actors
* linked places
* linked items
* linked cases/contracts/domain projects
* completion/failure time if available
* consequences
* summary

Archive should not clutter active Context.

But archive may matter later.

Examples:

A completed quest may affect reputation.

A breached contract may affect trust.

A failed domain project may leave ruins.

A solved case may create a title.

A completed labor project may improve holding.

⸻

27. Objective and Context behavior

Context should include objectives when relevant.

Context may include:

* active immediate quest
* current objective
* urgent deadline
* blocker
* active contract custody issue
* current labor/domain project if scene involves it
* case objective if investigating
* reward promise if relevant
* NPC obligation if interacting with them

Context should not include:

* every archived quest
* every old contract
* every inactive domain project
* every worker assignment when traveling elsewhere
* full quest history
* old reward logs

Good Context example:

“You promised Mara to find her missing ring. You have found the ring, but still need to return it to her.”

Good Context example:

“Mira has custody of your iron sword for repair and promised it by morning.”

Bad Context example:

“Active quests: 17 entries including all old tasks, archived missions, completed contracts, old rumors…”

Objective Context must be compact and current.

⸻

28. Objective and Story Card behavior

Story Cards should mirror stable important objectives.

Protected cards may include:

* Quest Log
* Domain & Contracts
* Investigation & Evidence
* Party & Companions if objective involves party
* Time & Travel if deadlines matter

Generated cards may be allowed for:

* important active quest
* important contract
* major domain project
* important case
* major faction/domain objective

Do not generate cards for:

* tiny errands
* vague hooks
* every offered task
* every worker order
* every minor payment
* every completed mundane transaction
* failed low-importance objectives
* old archive clutter

Card updates should happen when:

* quest accepted
* objective changes
* quest completed/failed
* contract terms change
* item custody changes
* domain project progresses meaningfully
* deadline changes
* blocker resolved
* objective archived

Cards mirror state.

Cards do not create objective truth.

⸻

29. Objective validation outcomes

All objective events use the five validation outcomes.

Accept

Use when objective state change is directly confirmed.

Examples:

* player accepts quest
* item delivered
* contract agreed
* workers begin task
* project reaches milestone
* quest completed
* contract fulfilled

Reject

Use when objective change is unsupported.

Examples:

* NPC only mentions problem
* player only considers helping
* rumor says objective completed
* quote describes completion
* work was planned but not started
* wrong item delivered

Pending

Use when objective may matter later.

Examples:

* quest hook
* possible job offer
* promised reward
* workers may be available
* project idea
* contract proposal
* possible deadline

Soft_fail

Use when real attempt makes partial progress but not full completion.

Examples:

* player searches but finds only clue
* workers repair part of wall
* delivery delayed but not failed
* negotiation fails but terms are clarified
* crafting service starts but item not complete

Hard_deny

Use when objective event comes from unsafe source.

Examples:

* pretend quest completion
* dream contract
* debug/card text
* fake reward
* impossible domain completion from one line
* junk objective name
* explicit failure parsed as success

30. Objective safety rules

30.1 No quest from every problem mention

An NPC saying “my ring is missing” is a hook, not necessarily accepted quest.

30.2 No acceptance without agreement or clear assignment

The player must agree, promise, be assigned, or otherwise take responsibility.

30.3 No completion from rumor

“People say you solved it” does not complete quest/case.

30.4 No reward before reward is granted

Promised reward is pending.

Physical reward requires handoff/payment.

30.5 No domain project from casual place description

“The wall is old” does not create repair project.

30.6 No labor group from vague help

“People offer to help” is not five hired workers unless count/commitment is clear.

30.7 No contract without terms

A contract needs parties and obligation.

Payment is common but not always required; terms are required.

30.8 No direct cross-owner reward

QuestReducer does not directly add coins/items/XP.

30.9 No duplicate completion/reward

OperationLedger prevents repeated objective rewards.

30.10 No silent archive loss

Completed/failed objectives should archive, not vanish.

30.11 No card-created objectives

Story Card text does not create active quests/contracts/domain projects.

30.12 No overactive Context

Only relevant active objectives should be injected.

34. Final Chunk 11 doctrine

Objectives need clear primary types.

Quests are adventure objectives.

Contracts are agreements with terms.

Labor is work done by workers or worker groups.

Domain projects are managed improvements to holdings, bases, infrastructure, or organizations.

Investigation cases are evidence-driven mysteries.

Crafting projects create or modify items.

Travel objectives move the player through the world.

One story situation can link several systems, but each truth has one owner.

A quest hook is not an accepted quest.

A promise is not a reward.

A price is not a transaction.

A worker offer is not hired labor.

A wall needing repair is not automatically a domain project.

Progress is not completion.

Completion requires confirmed fulfillment.

Failure requires clear evidence.

Rewards must be routed through the proper owner.

Archives preserve history without cluttering active play.

Context should show the current useful objective, not every old task.

Cards should mirror only stable important objectives.

The goal is to make the player’s goals, obligations, workers, projects, and rewards coherent without turning every passing conversation into system bloat.
