AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 10: Crafting, Materials, Recipes, Quality, Repair, and Workflows

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 9 defined inventory, equipment, ownership, custody, economy, and transactions.

Chunk 10 defines how items are made, repaired, improved, modified, consumed, and transformed.

This chunk answers:

What counts as crafting?

What counts as repair?

What counts as upgrading or altering an item?

How do materials work?

How do recipes work?

When are materials consumed?

How does item quality get decided?

How does crafting avoid instantly creating items from vague narration?

How do NPC crafting, hired labor, contracts, and domain projects connect?

How does crafting give progression without becoming farmable?

The central rule is:

Crafting is a workflow, not instant item generation.

Mentioning a forge does not create a sword.

Starting work does not create a finished item.

A finished item exists only when the story confirms completion and the required conditions are satisfied.

⸻

2. Core philosophy

Crafting should feel like meaningful work.

It should support:

* making items
* repairing items
* improving items
* modifying items
* enchanting items
* preparing consumables
* processing materials
* learning recipes
* experimenting
* failing productively
* commissioning NPCs
* using workshops
* using domain labor
* creating quest items
* creating evidence items
* creating tools for later scenes

Crafting should not:

* create items from every crafting-related phrase
* consume materials without confirmation
* upgrade gear for free
* ignore tools/workstations
* make perfect items from one vague output
* duplicate completed products
* bypass inventory ownership/custody
* treat every NPC service as player crafting
* turn ordinary labor into domain projects automatically

Crafting should be useful, but not so detailed that it slows normal AI Dungeon play.

⸻

3. Ownership boundaries

Crafting touches many systems, but each truth still has one owner.

CraftingReducer owns

* crafting attempts
* crafting progress
* known recipes
* recipe discovery
* recipe improvisation
* work stages
* crafting project status
* repair workflow status
* upgrade workflow status
* material requirement proposals
* crafting failure records
* quality result proposals
* crafting-related diagnostics

InventoryReducer / ItemAuthority owns

* actual item records
* material quantities
* consumed materials
* new crafted items
* repaired item condition
* upgraded item records
* item custody
* item ownership
* item access
* equipment effects

CraftingReducer may propose item creation, material consumption, item repair, or item alteration.

InventoryReducer commits actual item changes.

EconomyReducer owns

* material prices
* service prices
* crafting fees
* sale value
* repair costs
* paid commissions

ContractReducer owns

* NPC crafting agreements
* repair contracts
* commission terms
* payment obligations
* delivery deadlines
* custody obligations
* breach/fulfillment

LaborReducer / DomainReducer owns

* worker groups
* workshops
* domain production
* large projects
* repeated manufacturing
* base/holding improvements

ProgressionReducer owns

* Crafting Domain XP
* relevant Stat XP
* Level XP from major creations
* anti-farm reward control

CardSync owns

* generated item cards only after item state is stable and important
* protected inventory/crafting summaries if needed

The rule:

CraftingReducer manages the process. InventoryReducer owns the item truth.

⸻

4. What counts as crafting

Crafting is the process of creating something new from materials, tools, knowledge, time, and effort.

Examples:

* forging an iron dagger
* brewing a healing potion
* carving a wooden charm
* sewing a cloak
* preparing poison
* cooking travel rations
* inscribing a rune plate
* building a trap
* assembling arrows
* making bandages
* refining mana crystals
* shaping leather armor
* constructing a lockpick set

Crafting usually requires:

* intent or assignment
* materials
* tools
* workspace or station
* recipe or improvisation
* time
* skill/domain/stat support
* result confirmation

⸻

5. What counts as repair

Repair is restoring an existing item’s condition.

Examples:

* mending torn cloak
* sharpening chipped sword
* reforging cracked blade
* patching leather armor
* repairing shield straps
* re-stringing a bow
* fixing broken lockpicks
* stabilizing cracked mana crystal
* cleansing curse damage from an amulet

Repair should reference an existing item.

If there is no existing item, the event may be invalid or become crafting instead.

Repair usually requires:

* item access or custody
* correct tools
* materials or replacement parts
* skill
* time
* possible cost
* completion confirmation

Repair changes item condition.

It does not necessarily improve item quality beyond its original quality unless upgraded.

⸻

6. What counts as upgrading

Upgrade means improving an existing item beyond its current baseline.

Examples:

* reinforcing a shield
* sharpening a sword beyond normal
* adding silver edge to a blade
* enchanting a cloak
* adding hidden pockets
* improving potion potency
* adding runes to armor
* converting a staff into a focus
* strengthening a bowstring
* fitting a weapon to the player’s grip

Upgrade differs from repair.

Repair restores.

Upgrade improves.

An item can be repaired and upgraded in the same project, but those should be distinct effects.

Upgrade usually requires stronger validation than repair because it changes item power.

⸻

7. What counts as alteration

Alteration means changing an item’s form, function, or fit.

Examples:

* resizing armor
* dyeing cloak
* changing sword grip
* turning a broken spear into a staff
* modifying a trap
* cutting a cloak into bandages
* converting raw herbs into poultice
* removing a crest from armor
* disguising a weapon as a cane

Alteration may:

* preserve item identity
* create a new item
* consume the old item
* split one item into several
* combine multiple items

InventoryReducer should commit the final item changes.

CraftingReducer should manage the workflow.

⸻

8. What counts as processing materials

Material processing turns raw materials into usable crafting inputs.

Examples:

* tanning hide into leather
* smelting ore into ingots
* drying herbs
* grinding crystal dust
* cutting timber
* weaving cloth
* purifying water
* distilling alcohol
* extracting venom
* cleaning monster parts

Processing may produce:

* refined materials
* waste
* quality changes
* spoilage
* dangerous byproducts
* crafting XP

Processing should not create finished gear unless the output directly supports it.

⸻

9. Crafting workflow

Crafting should follow a lifecycle.

Stage 1 — Intent

The player wants to craft, repair, upgrade, alter, or process something.

Example:

“I want to forge a dagger.”

Intent alone does not create the dagger.

Stage 2 — Requirement check

The system checks whether required pieces exist or are implied.

Requirements may include:

* materials
* item to repair/upgrade
* tools
* station
* recipe
* knowledge
* time
* safety
* assistant/NPC
* heat/fire/water
* magical resource
* workspace access

If requirements are missing, the project may become pending, blocked, or improvised.

Stage 3 — Work started

The player begins the work.

This may consume time, EP, MP, or reserve materials.

It may not consume all materials yet unless the story says so.

Stage 4 — Progress made

Work advances.

Progress may be partial.

The system may track progress percent, stage count, or narrative milestone.

Examples:

* blade shaped
* herbs prepared
* rune circle drawn
* leather cut
* shield cleaned and braced

Stage 5 — Complication or failure

Something may go wrong.

Examples:

* material cracks
* potion curdles
* blade warps
* rune misaligns
* tool breaks
* fire too cold
* player exhausted
* magical backlash
* recipe incomplete

Failure can be productive.

It may teach, consume some materials, or reveal a requirement.

Stage 6 — Completion

The item, repair, upgrade, alteration, or processed material is finished.

This is when final inventory changes should commit.

Completion must be confirmed by narration or command.

Stage 7 — Quality assignment

The system assigns quality based on evidence.

Quality may be:

* crude
* poor
* common
* sturdy
* fine
* masterwork
* exceptional
* legendary
* flawed
* unstable
* cursed
* enchanted

Quality should reflect skill, materials, tools, time, luck, risk, and story result.

Stage 8 — Inventory update

InventoryReducer commits:

* new item
* material consumption
* condition repair
* quality update
* item alteration
* ownership/custody
* access state

CraftingReducer does not directly own final item truth.

Stage 9 — Progression and dirty updates

ProgressionReducer may award:

* Crafting Domain XP
* INT XP
* ATK/DEF/SPD XP if physical labor matters
* Magic Domain XP if magical crafting matters
* Level XP for major creations

Dirty queues mark inventory, crafting, equipment, Context, and cards if relevant.

11. Materials

Materials are items used to craft, repair, upgrade, or process.

Examples:

* iron ore
* iron ingot
* timber
* leather
* cloth
* herbs
* crystal dust
* mana crystal
* wolf pelt
* bone
* venom sac
* silver thread
* glass vial
* oil
* charcoal
* rune ink

Materials can be:

* raw
* refined
* rare
* unstable
* magical
* perishable
* cursed
* contaminated
* high quality
* low quality

Material state should include:

* quantity
* quality
* condition
* storage location
* ownership
* custody
* access
* spoilage risk if relevant
* crafting tags

Materials are inventory items.

InventoryReducer owns material quantities.

CraftingReducer may reserve or request consumption.

⸻

12. Material consumption timing

One of the most important design decisions is when materials are consumed.

Recommended model:

Planning stage

No materials consumed.

The system may check requirements.

Work start

Materials may be reserved.

Some expendable materials may be consumed if the narration says work actually begins.

Example:

“You pour the oil into the crucible.”

Oil may be consumed.

Progress stage

Materials may be partially consumed.

Example:

“You cut the leather panels.”

Leather may be consumed or converted into partially finished component.

Failure stage

Some, all, or no materials may be lost depending on failure.

Example:

Potion curdles.

Herbs and vial contents may be consumed.

Example:

Sword shaping goes poorly.

Ingot may become flawed blank, not vanish.

Completion stage

Remaining required materials are consumed or transformed into the finished product.

The rule:

Do not consume all materials at intent unless the story clearly says they were used.

⸻

13. Material reservation

For longer projects, materials may be reserved.

Reserved means:

* the materials still exist
* they are tied to a project
* they should not be freely used elsewhere without releasing them
* they may be consumed later
* they may be returned if project is cancelled before use

Example:

The player prepares iron ingots, leather strips, and charcoal for a shield reinforcement.

Those materials may be reserved.

If the project is cancelled before work starts, they remain.

If work begins, some may be consumed.

Material reservation prevents double-use while avoiding premature deletion.

14. Recipes

A recipe is knowledge of how to make something.

Recipe records should track:

* recipe name
* output type
* required materials
* required tools
* required station
* required domain rank or skill
* time estimate
* difficulty
* possible qualities
* failure risks
* variations
* known by whom
* source
* confidence

Recipe knowledge can be:

* unknown
* rumored
* partial
* improvised
* learned
* mastered
* corrupted
* secret
* forbidden

Examples:

* healing poultice recipe
* iron dagger recipe
* mana crystal focus recipe
* fire resistance potion recipe
* wolf-hide cloak recipe
* silvered blade process
* binding rune formula

⸻

15. Recipe discovery

Recipes can be learned from:

* books
* teachers
* experimentation
* observation
* old notes
* guild training
* reverse engineering
* magical insight
* domain project research
* quest rewards
* cultural background
* species memory

Recipe discovery should be validated.

Mentioning a recipe name does not mean the player knows it.

Example:

“The book describes a potion of fire resistance.”

Possible result:

* recipe lead/pending
* partial recipe knowledge if read and understood
* not automatically mastered

Example:

“Mira teaches you the steps to brew frost salve.”

Possible result:

* recipe learned or partial learned depending narration

⸻

16. Improvised crafting

The player may try to craft without a known recipe.

Improvised crafting should be possible but riskier.

Improvisation depends on:

* INT
* Crafting Domain
* relevant material knowledge
* similar known recipes
* tools/station
* quality of materials
* time
* risk tolerance
* LCK
* teacher/assistant help

Improvised results may be:

* crude
* unstable
* partial
* functional but flawed
* failed
* dangerous
* surprisingly good with strong support

Improvised crafting should not easily create high-quality or magical items without strong story support.

Examples:

Specialist tools

Workstations

19. Crafting progress model

Crafting progress can be tracked in one of three ways.

19.1 Simple completion

Use for small items.

Example:

The player makes a bandage.

One confirmed output can complete it.

19.2 Stage-based progress

Use for moderate projects.

Example:

Forging a sword:

* gather materials
* heat ingot
* shape blade
* quench
* grind edge
* fit hilt
* finish

Each stage may require time and resources.

19.3 Project progress percentage

Use for large/domain projects.

Example:

Repairing a wall:

* 35% complete

This belongs more to domain/labor, but crafting can link to it.

Recommended rule:

Use simple completion for small personal crafting.

Use stages for important items.

Use progress percentages for domain-scale work.

⸻

20. Crafting failure

Failure should not be all-or-nothing unless appropriate.

Failure outcomes:

* no progress
* partial progress
* material wasted
* material damaged
* item flawed
* item unstable
* tool damaged
* time lost
* EP/MP cost
* injury
* condition
* hazard created
* inferior item created
* recipe insight gained
* requirement discovered

Examples:

Potion failure:

* herbs consumed
* potion ruined
* alchemy insight gained
* maybe fumes cause condition

Sword forging failure:

* blade warps
* ingot becomes flawed blank
* more work needed
* no finished sword

Rune failure:

* MP backlash
* crystal cracks
* unstable enchantment
* clue about missing symbol

Failure should feel like consequence, not random punishment.

⸻

21. Partial success

Partial success is important.

Examples:

* potion is weak but usable
* sword is crude but functional
* armor patch holds temporarily
* trap works once
* rune activates but drains extra MP
* cloak is repaired but ugly
* crystal focus works but is unstable

Partial success may create:

* lower-quality item
* temporary item
* flawed condition
* unstable tag
* higher cost
* lower durability
* limited uses

Partial success should be especially common when:

* recipe is partial
* tools are improvised
* materials are poor
* player is low skill
* project is rushed
* player is exhausted
* magic is unstable

⸻

22. Quality calculation

Quality should be assigned from story evidence and system factors.

Quality factors:

* material quality
* tool/station quality
* recipe knowledge
* Crafting Domain rank
* relevant stats, especially INT
* relevant abilities/talents
* time spent
* assistance
* environment
* failure/complication
* resource state
* LCK
* narration wording

Quality should not be random only.

Narration matters.

Example:

“You carefully temper the blade under Mira’s guidance until it rings clear.”

Likely quality:

* sturdy or fine, depending skill/materials

Example:

“You hammer the scrap metal into a rough blade by firelight.”

Likely quality:

* crude or poor

Example:

“The rune flares wildly, unstable but powerful.”

Likely quality:

* unstable enchanted

⸻

23. Quality levels

Recommended quality levels:

Ruined

Not useful except scrap.

Crude

Functional but poor.

Higher failure risk.

Lower value.

Poor

Usable but unreliable or weak.

Common

Ordinary functional item.

Sturdy

Reliable and durable.

Fine

Better than average.

Good balance, durability, or potency.

Masterwork

Excellent workmanship.

Strong mechanical and story value.

Exceptional

Rarely crafted.

May have special features.

Legendary

Story-defining quality.

Requires major support.

Flawed

Can apply alongside another quality.

Example:

fine but flawed.

Unstable

Works, but unpredictable or risky.

Common for magical experiments.

Enchanted

Magically empowered.

Needs effect and limits.

Cursed

Has harmful or binding property.

Needs condition and removal rules.

Quality should affect value, durability, effectiveness, and card eligibility.

24. Repair workflow

Repair should use a specific workflow.

Stage 1 — Damage identified

The item condition is known.

Example:

Shield is cracked.

Stage 2 — Repair requirements determined

May require:

* tools
* materials
* station
* skill
* money
* NPC
* time

Stage 3 — Item custody confirmed

Who has the item?

If NPC repairs it, custody may transfer.

Stage 4 — Work begins

Time/resources/materials may be spent.

Stage 5 — Progress or complication

Repair may advance, fail, or reveal hidden damage.

Stage 6 — Repair complete

InventoryReducer updates condition.

Stage 7 — Item returned or stored

Custody/access updates.

Repair can restore condition.

Upgrade is separate.

Example:

Cracked shield repaired to good condition.

That does not automatically make it masterwork.

⸻

25. Repair outcomes

Repair outcomes:

* fully repaired
* partially repaired
* patched temporarily
* improved
* failed
* worsened
* transformed
* destroyed
* revealed hidden damage
* cursed/tainted if magical repair fails

Temporary repair should have limits.

Example:

“The patch will hold for one fight.”

That should create temporary condition or durability note.

⸻

26. Upgrade workflow

Upgrade means improving item performance or adding feature.

Upgrade stages:

* choose upgrade goal
* verify item
* verify materials
* verify tools/station
* verify skill/recipe
* start modification
* progress
* complication
* completion
* quality/effect assignment
* inventory update

Upgrade examples:

* silvered edge
* reinforced shield
* hidden blade mechanism
* enchanted rune
* improved grip
* lighter armor fitting
* potion potency boost
* sharpened master edge

Upgrade should require stronger support than repair.

Do not upgrade from vague praise.

Example:

“Your sword feels better.”

Not enough.

“Mira reforges the edge with silver and quenches it in moonwater.”

Strong upgrade evidence.

27. Enchanting and magical crafting

Enchanting is crafting plus magic.

It may require:

* MP
* magical domain
* recipe/ritual
* rare materials
* focus item
* time
* risk
* ritual circle
* alignment/lunar/elemental conditions
* magical backlash handling

Enchanting outcomes:

* stable enchantment
* weak enchantment
* unstable enchantment
* cursed item
* failed ritual
* temporary charge
* passive item effect
* active item ability
* attunement requirement

Enchanting should always define:

* effect
* cost
* trigger
* limit
* duration
* recharge
* risk
* ownership/custody
* whether player can use it

Magical items should not become limitless powers without limits.

28. Consumable crafting

Consumables include:

* potions
* poisons
* salves
* bombs
* scrolls
* traps
* rations
* bandages
* talismans
* oils
* ammunition

Consumables should track quantity and uses.

Crafting consumables usually consumes materials at completion or batch stage.

Batch crafting should be supported.

Example:

The player brews three minor healing potions.

Inventory result:

* healing potion quantity +3

Duplicate output must not add six.

Consumables should have:

* potency
* quality
* expiration if relevant
* risk if unstable
* use effect
* use conditions

⸻

29. Crafting and time

Crafting should cost time.

Time cost depends on:

* difficulty
* tools
* assistants
* domain rank
* workspace
* materials
* rush
* failure
* project size

Examples:

* bandage: minutes
* simple meal: minutes/hour
* potion: hour or more
* dagger: hours/day
* armor: days/weeks
* artifact: quest-scale

TimeReducer owns time passage.

CraftingReducer proposes time costs.

If time passes, deadlines and recovery ticks may also occur.

Do not let crafting time apply twice on repeated output.

⸻

30. Crafting and resources

Crafting may cost resources.

Common costs:

* EP for physical labor
* MP for magical crafting
* HP only for blood magic, dangerous backlash, injury, or sacrifice
* materials
* tool durability
* money
* time

Resource costs depend on:

* difficulty
* duration
* environment
* fatigue
* ability use
* rushing
* failure
* assistance

Example:

Forging for hours costs EP.

Enchanting costs MP.

Blood rune costs HP if explicitly using blood/life force.

⸻

31. Crafting and progression

Crafting can reward progression.

Possible rewards:

* Crafting Domain XP
* INT XP
* ATK XP for heavy physical work
* DEF XP for endurance/long labor
* Magic Domain XP for enchanting
* Ability evidence for crafting-related powers
* Level XP for major creations or quest/domain milestones

Reward depends on:

* meaningful work
* difficulty
* risk
* time
* material cost
* success/partial/failure
* novelty
* story relevance
* anti-farm rules

Small repetitive crafting should not farm infinite XP.

Example:

Making ten simple bandages after mastering it:

* little XP

Forging a first proper sword under pressure:

* meaningful Crafting Domain XP
* INT XP
* possible Level XP if story-relevant

⸻

32. Crafting and anti-farm rules

Crafting is highly farmable, so it needs safeguards.

No or reduced XP from:

* repeated trivial item
* no-cost crafting
* duplicate output
* vague “you craft something”
* crafting with no materials/time
* low-risk mass repetition
* same recipe spam beyond usefulness

Reduced rewards if:

* same recipe repeated
* same context
* no new difficulty
* no new quality goal
* no risk
* no meaningful result

Full or better rewards if:

* new recipe
* harder item
* rare material
* teacher challenge
* meaningful deadline
* dangerous environment
* improved quality
* quest/domain relevance
* first successful version
* major repair or upgrade

⸻

33. NPC crafting

NPC crafting is not player crafting.

If an NPC crafts for the player, the main systems are:

* ContractReducer
* EconomyReducer
* InventoryReducer
* possibly CraftingReducer for progress/status

Example:

“Mira agrees to repair your sword for 5 silver.”

This is primarily:

* contract
* economy
* item custody
* repair workflow

The player should not gain full Crafting Domain XP for Mira doing the work.

The player may gain:

* social/contract progress
* maybe crafting insight if observing or assisting
* no direct crafting mastery if uninvolved

If the player assists Mira:

* player may gain Crafting Domain XP
* work may progress faster
* failure/success may depend partly on player stats

⸻

34. Crafting contracts

A crafting contract is an agreement for someone to make, repair, upgrade, or deliver an item.

Contract should track:

* item
* service
* crafter
* customer
* payment
* materials supplied
* custody
* deadline
* quality expectation
* failure terms
* current status

Examples:

* repair sword by morning
* craft silvered dagger for 10 silver
* brew antidote if herbs supplied
* enchant cloak under moonlight
* make 20 arrows by dawn

If NPC has custody of player item, inventory access must update.

If NPC fails or steals item, contract may breach.

⸻

35. Domain-scale crafting

Large crafting or construction becomes domain/labor.

Examples:

* build a wall
* repair a bridge
* arm the militia
* produce 100 arrows
* construct a workshop
* outfit a caravan
* reinforce a keep gate
* forge weapons for a guard unit

This should not be handled as one personal crafting item.

Domain-scale projects involve:

* DomainReducer
* LaborReducer
* EconomyReducer
* InventoryReducer
* TimeReducer
* possibly CraftingReducer for recipe/production logic

Rule:

Personal crafting makes items.

Domain crafting manages production or infrastructure.

⸻

36. Crafting and investigation

Items can be evidence.

Crafting can affect investigation when:

* item is forged
* evidence is repaired
* clue is analyzed
* poison is identified
* weapon is matched to wound
* document is restored
* fake item is detected
* tool marks are studied

InvestigationReducer owns evidence meaning.

CraftingReducer may propose analysis/repair.

InventoryReducer owns the item itself.

Example:

The player repairs a torn letter.

InventoryReducer updates letter condition.

InvestigationReducer may add readable evidence if text is revealed.

⸻

37. Crafting and abilities

Some abilities interact with crafting.

Examples:

* Rune Inscription
* Alchemical Sense
* Mana Weaving
* Fire Shaping
* Metal Whisper
* Quick Stitch
* Poison Brewing
* Spirit Binding

Ability use in crafting may affect:

* quality
* time
* failure risk
* material efficiency
* magical effect
* cost
* backlash

AbilityReducer owns ability growth.

CraftingReducer owns crafting process.

InventoryReducer owns final items.

ProgressionReducer owns XP.

38. Crafting Context behavior

Context should include crafting facts when relevant.

Good Context examples:

“You are midway through repairing your cracked shield. You have leather straps and a hammer, but no proper forge.”

“Mira currently has custody of your iron sword for repair. It is due by morning.”

“The fire resistance potion recipe is only partially known; failure risk is high.”

“You have enough frost herbs for one antidote attempt.”

Bad Context examples:

Dumping every known recipe every turn.

Listing every material count in unrelated combat.

Explaining the entire crafting state when the player is traveling.

Context should include crafting when:

* player is crafting
* item is unavailable because of repair/custody
* quest requires crafted item
* materials are relevant
* recipe/risk matters
* deadline exists
* damaged equipment matters

39. Crafting Story Card behavior

Crafting should rarely generate cards by itself.

Cards may be appropriate for:

* major crafted artifact
* important named weapon
* key quest item
* magical item
* cursed item
* domain workshop
* major crafting contract
* important recipe collection
* unique crafting project

Do not create cards for:

* every bandage
* every potion
* every material
* every recipe hint
* every failed craft
* every repair step
* every mundane item

Protected summary cards may include:

* Inventory Overview
* Equipment Overview
* Domain & Contracts
* Abilities Overview if crafting ability matters

Generated item cards should update only if the item is stable and important.

40. Crafting validation outcomes

Crafting uses the same five validation outcomes.

Accept

Use when crafting progress, completion, repair, or material use is clearly confirmed.

Example:

“You finish stitching the cloak, and the torn seam holds.”

Accepted:

* repair progress/complete
* condition update

Reject

Use when crafting did not happen or is unsupported.

Example:

“You think about making a potion.”

Rejected:

* potion creation

Pending

Use when crafting is planned or possible.

Example:

“You set the herbs aside to brew later.”

Pending:

* planned craft
* materials reserved maybe, if explicit

Soft_fail

Use when real work occurs but result is incomplete or failed productively.

Example:

“The potion curdles, but you learn the mixture needed less heat.”

Soft-fail:

* materials may be consumed
* recipe insight gained
* no finished potion

Hard_deny

Use when crafting event comes from non-canon or unsafe source.

Examples:

* pretend crafting
* dream crafting
* quoted instruction
* card text
* debug example
* impossible artifact creation with no support
* junk item name

41. Crafting safety rules

41.1 No item from intent alone

“I craft a sword” does not create sword.

41.2 No material consumption from planning alone

Thinking about crafting does not consume materials.

41.3 No finished product without completion

Starting work is not finishing work.

41.4 No crafting without required access

Cannot repair sword if sword is with someone else unless working there or custody is transferred.

41.5 No infinite material use

Materials cannot be used twice.

41.6 No duplicate finished item

Repeated output does not create duplicate product.

41.7 No free high quality

Masterwork or legendary items require strong support.

41.8 No NPC work as player mastery

Player does not gain full crafting XP for NPC doing all work.

41.9 No domain-scale projects as personal craft

Building a wall is domain/labor, not one personal craft.

41.10 No card spam

Ordinary crafted items do not get cards.

41.11 No quality from vague praise

“The item feels nice” is not enough for masterwork.

41.12 No repair without item

Repair requires existing item or clear target.

45. Final Chunk 10 doctrine

Crafting is a process.

Repair restores.

Upgrade improves.

Alteration changes form or function.

Processing transforms materials.

Materials are inventory.

Recipes are knowledge.

Tools and stations matter.

Time matters.

Resources matter.

Quality comes from evidence, skill, materials, tools, time, and story result.

Failure can teach.

Partial success can create flawed but useful results.

NPC crafting is contract/economy/custody first, not player mastery.

Domain-scale production belongs to labor/domain systems.

CraftingReducer manages workflow.

InventoryReducer owns actual items and materials.

ProgressionReducer owns XP.

ContractReducer owns NPC agreements.

CardSync mirrors only important stable crafted items.

The goal is not to simulate every hammer blow.

The goal is to make created, repaired, upgraded, and magical items feel earned, persistent, and believable.
