AIDRPG Design Bible — Chunk 28: Economy, Prices, Wages, Domain Costs, Labor Output, Project Scaling, and Anti-Exploit Rules

1. Purpose of this chunk

Chunk 28 defines how AIDRPG handles money, prices, wages, workers, foremen, project costs, domain expenses, market logic, and economic validation.

Earlier chunks defined:

* level scaling
* race/species scaling
* stats/resources
* XP and progression
* enemy scaling
* combat math
* ability costs
* absorption/anime growth
* crafting and item creation

Chunk 28 answers:

How much should things cost?

How much does a sword cost?

How much does armor cost?

How much does a potion cost?

How much does a worker earn?

How much can workers build in a day?

How much does it cost to repair a sword?

How much does it cost to build a wall, road, house, workshop, keep, or fortress?

How does AIDRPG stop infinite-money exploits?

How does the system validate transactions?

How does the economy scale from early village play to high-level kingdom/domain play?

The central rule is:

Economy exists to make resources, labor, ownership, and large projects believable.

It should support gameplay, not drown the player in accounting.

⸻

2. Core philosophy

AI Dungeon often creates economic events freely.

The player may write:

“I buy a sword.”

“I sell the wolf pelts.”

“I hire five workers.”

“I pay Mara to repair my sword.”

“I build a wall around my camp.”

“I open a shop.”

“I mine silver.”

“I sell dragon scales.”

“I hire guards.”

“I start a mercenary company.”

“I build a fortress.”

“I pay the villagers to gather materials.”

“I use my gold to fund a guild.”

“I create infinite coins with magic.”

AIDRPG must support economic play while preventing:

* free item creation
* free money generation
* duplicate transactions
* buying items without paying
* selling the same item repeatedly
* workers doing impossible amounts of labor
* instant buildings
* untracked debt
* inconsistent currency
* infinite crafting profit loops
* high-level players trivializing every economy without consequence
* AI Dungeon randomly awarding huge money without validation

Economy should be flexible, setting-aware, and scalable.

A village economy should feel different from a capital city economy.

A copper economy should feel different from a divine/cosmic artifact economy.

4. Currency model

AIDRPG should support a simple default currency model.

Recommended default:

100 copper = 1 silver
100 silver = 1 gold
100 gold = 1 platinum

So:

1 silver = 100 copper
1 gold = 10,000 copper
1 platinum = 1,000,000 copper

This gives enough scale for:

* cheap meals
* simple tools
* weapons
* armor
* property
* domain projects
* high-level wealth

Alternative settings may use:

* coins only
* credits
* spirit stones
* mana crystals
* barter
* shells
* gems
* favor
* ration tokens
* gold/silver/copper with different conversion
* paper notes
* guild credit
* divine favor

But the script needs one default.

The default should be copper/silver/gold/platinum unless the scenario defines another system.

5. Currency display

Player-facing currency should be readable.

Example:

Currency:
3 gold, 42 silver, 18 copper

Internally, store currency as base copper value to avoid arithmetic bugs.

Example:

3 gold = 30,000 copper
42 silver = 4,200 copper
18 copper = 18 copper
Total = 34,218 copper

Display can convert back.

This prevents transaction errors.

7. Baseline price philosophy

Prices should be approximate.

AIDRPG should not need exact real-world medieval economy accuracy.

Prices should be believable enough to support gameplay.

Prices depend on:

* settlement size
* supply
* demand
* rarity
* danger
* season
* war
* famine
* taxes
* quality
* reputation
* faction relationship
* legality
* distance from source
* magical nature
* urgency
* bargaining
* scarcity
* currency inflation
* world setting

Use price bands, not fixed universal numbers.

A sword in a village may cost more than in a smithing city.

Healing potions may be common in a high-magic capital and rare in a poor frontier town.

Dragon scales may be priceless in one place and illegal in another.

15. Sale value

Selling an item usually returns less than buy price.

Default sale value:

Situation	Sale Value
common item to normal merchant	40–60% market price
high-demand item	60–90%
rare item to specialist	70–120%
damaged item	reduced by condition
stolen item	10–50% and legal risk
illegal item	black-market risk
unknown artifact	appraisal needed
desperate sale	10–40%
auction	variable, may exceed value

Players should not buy and sell same item for profit unless market manipulation, trade routes, or business systems support it.

Unknown items require appraisal.

Appraisal depends on:

* INT
* relevant domain
* merchant skill
* magic detection
* item rarity
* deception risk
* condition
* provenance
* time

Appraisal outcomes:

* accurate value
* rough range
* mistaken value
* hidden curse missed
* fake identified
* origin discovered
* buyer interest found

Appraisal services cost money.

Rare items may need specialists.

A village merchant may not know the value of dragon bone.

17. Transaction lifecycle

Every transaction should follow a lifecycle.

17.1 Offer

Seller offers item/service.

17.2 Price

Price is determined or negotiated.

17.3 Availability

Item/service must exist.

17.4 Affordability

Buyer must have accessible funds.

17.5 Consent

Owner must agree to sell or transaction must be theft/confiscation.

17.6 Transfer

Currency and item/service change hands.

17.7 Commit

EconomyReducer updates money.

InventoryReducer updates item ownership/custody.

17.8 Receipt/Audit

OperationLedger records transaction.

This prevents one-sided purchases.

19. Buying rules

Buying requires:

* item/service exists or can be commissioned
* seller has authority to sell
* player has accessible funds
* item is legal/available
* price agreed
* inventory/custody updated
* money removed
* transaction deduped

If player lacks funds:

* reject purchase
* offer debt/credit only if seller allows
* barter possible if item offered

If item is unavailable:

* reject immediate purchase
* allow order/commission/search

If item is too rare:

* require quest, auction, specialist, or faction access

⸻

20. Selling rules

Selling requires:

* player owns or can legally sell item
* item is accessible
* buyer exists and wants/can afford item
* price agreed
* item custody transferred
* player receives money
* item removed from inventory or ownership changed
* transaction deduped

Cannot sell:

* absent item
* borrowed item
* item already sold
* item in someone else’s custody unless authorized
* fake duplicate
* quest item if protected, unless story allows
* cursed item without disclosure, unless deception path

Selling stolen items may trigger legal/reputation risk.

⸻

21. Barter rules

Barter uses item-for-item or service-for-service value.

Barter requires:

* both parties agree
* both sides own/access items
* values are plausible
* social/reputation context supports trade
* transfer committed

Barter can include:

* goods
* labor
* favors
* protection
* information
* titles
* contracts
* future payment
* hostages/collateral
* magical oaths

ContractReducer may track deferred barter.

Example:

“I repair your roof if you give me the old map.”

Creates contract if not completed immediately.

32. Project cost formula

Domain project cost includes:

Total Project Cost =
Material Cost
+ Labor Cost
+ Specialist Cost
+ Tool/Station Cost
+ Transport Cost
+ Security Cost
+ Permit/Tax/Bribe Cost
+ Waste/Risk Margin

Waste/risk margin:

Situation	Margin
well-planned safe project	+5–10%
normal project	+10–25%
rushed project	+25–50%
dangerous frontier project	+25–100%
magical/experimental project	+50–200%
corrupt/hostile region	+20–100%

This prevents underpricing large projects.

Daily Progress =
Total Worker Units
× Tool Modifier
× Foreman Modifier
× Morale Modifier
× Weather Modifier
× Safety Modifier
× Material Availability Modifier
× Skill Match Modifier

If materials missing:

Material Availability Modifier = 0

If only partial materials:

Material Availability Modifier = 0.25–0.75

Example:

10 laborers, 2 masons.

* laborers: 10 WU
* masons: 6 WU
* total: 16 WU
* good foreman ×1.15
* rain ×0.75
* adequate tools ×1

Progress:

16 × 1.15 × 0.75 = 13.8 WU/day

Round as needed.

76. Economy safeguards

76.1 Money changes need a source

No unexplained currency gain/loss.

76.2 Transactions need both sides

Buyer, seller, item/service, and price must exist or be reasonably inferred.

76.3 Accessible funds matter

Money in distant treasury is not purse money.

76.4 Ownership matters

The player cannot sell what they do not own or control.

76.5 Custody matters

An item with a smith cannot be sold or used unless retrieved or authorized.

76.6 Availability matters

Rare items are not always for sale.

76.7 Condition and quality affect value

Damaged items sell for less.

76.8 Sale value is not buy value

No default buy/sell profit loop.

76.9 Market saturation prevents infinite sales

Small markets cannot buy endless goods.

76.10 Workers require wages, tools, materials, and time

No free labor.

76.11 Foremen matter for large projects

Large unorganized projects are inefficient.

76.12 Domain income requires assets

No income without holdings/workers/market.

76.13 Domain assets require maintenance

Buildings, guards, mines, wards, and workers cost money.

76.14 Magic can reduce labor but has costs

No free instant city-building without mythic support.

76.15 Contracts must be tracked

Deferred payments and obligations cannot vanish.

76.16 Quest rewards pay once

Promises are not payment.

76.17 Story Cards do not create money

Cards mirror state.

76.18 AI narration is validated

AI cannot randomly grant impossible wealth.

76.19 Duplicate transactions are blocked

OperationLedger prevents repeated money/item transfer.

76.20 Every major economic result should be auditable

The player should know what was paid, earned, owed, sold, bought, or blocked.

78. Final Chunk 28 doctrine

Economy is the structure that makes money, labor, materials, services, and domains believable.

Currency should be tracked in a stable base unit.

Prices should use setting-aware bands.

Items require availability.

Purchases require accessible funds.

Sales require ownership and buyers.

Sale value is not buy value.

Workers require wages.

Foremen improve productivity.

Projects require Work Units, materials, tools, specialists, time, and safety.

Domain assets create income only when they actually exist and operate.

Domain assets also require maintenance.

Magic can speed labor, but magic has costs, risks, and limits.

Large projects need logistics.

NPC services can change item custody.

Contracts track deferred obligations.

Debt matters.

Market saturation prevents infinite selling.

Crafting profit loops are controlled by material costs, labor, demand, and market size.

Stolen or illegal goods create risk.

Quest rewards pay once.

Story Cards do not create money.

AI narration is validated.

OperationLedger prevents duplicate currency, item, wage, and reward changes.

The goal is not to turn AI Dungeon into an accounting simulator.

The goal is to make economic choices meaningful, prevent exploits, support crafting and domain play, and make the world react believably when the player buys, sells, hires, builds, invests, owes, earns, or rules.
