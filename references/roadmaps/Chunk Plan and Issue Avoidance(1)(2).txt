The hard blockers are not mysterious. They come from a small set of platform and design pressures that push creators toward shortcuts:

* AI Dungeon scripting only gives you Library / Input / Context / Output on supported scenarios, and each hook runs in an isolated sandbox with a 16 MB memory limit and 2-second timeout.  
* Story Cards are trigger-based, only enter context when their triggers appear, and if a trigger happens in the AI’s current output, that card does not help until the next output. Also, the AI only sees the Entry text, not the card name, and it does not “know Story Cards exist.”  
* Plot Essentials is always considered, but the official guidance is to keep it dense and concise and only include what matters right now, because context is limited.  
* Changes to state.memory made in onOutput do not affect the AI until the next player action, which makes “immediate correction” harder than it first appears.  
* The public scripts themselves were built around different priorities: Auto-Cards focused on “object permanence” through automatic Story Card writing and compatibility, while Inner Self focused on NPC minds, agentic thought maintenance, and universal compatibility, not hard mechanics.  

Below is the real safety-net list: the reasons people stopped short, and the exact problems we should expect.

⸻

1. The platform strongly rewards “good enough memory tricks” over full simulation

A lot of creators stop at:

* auto-generating Story Cards,
* injecting a small context packet,
* or storing a few numbers in state

because that already gives a noticeable improvement without building a full engine.

Why they stopped there:

* It is far cheaper in hook time and complexity.
* It works on more scenarios and for more users.
* It avoids fighting context limits every turn.  

What this means for us:

* The jump from “helpful memory script” to “persistent RPG simulation layer” is not incremental. It is a big complexity leap.

⸻

2. Story Cards are great for long memory, but bad for immediate scene truth

This is one of the biggest reasons systems feel almost-right but not actually consistent.

Because Story Cards only trigger when their triggers appear, and an output-triggered card does not affect the same output, a script cannot rely on cards alone to immediately preserve:

* building damage,
* room condition,
* current visible gear,
* today’s exact time,
* or a newly formed ability meaning.  

So many scripts stop at “better long-term memory” instead of building a separate current-scene state layer.

Complication for us:

* If we get lazy and trust cards too much, the world will still feel delayed and inconsistent.

⸻

3. Freeform parsing is easy to start and hard to make reliable

This is the TAS-style problem in one sentence.

It is easy to say:

* detect damage from prose,
* detect a new skill from repeated wording,
* detect a gained item from story text,
* detect building damage from narration

But broad prose heuristics run into three constant problems:

A. Ambiguity

The same wording can mean:

* actual success,
* attempted action,
* metaphor,
* rumor,
* hypothetical,
* incomplete consequence

B. Over-triggering

A parser starts “seeing” things that did not really happen.

C. Under-triggering

Players phrase things creatively, and the system misses obvious real events.

That is why earlier systems often either:

* stayed too shallow,
* or became brittle and noisy.

Complication for us:

* We need a proposal → validation → save pipeline, not direct prose-to-canon.

⸻

4. The more freeform the system is, the harder balancing becomes

Your design explicitly wants:

* level 1–1600,
* level not equal to mastery,
* specialization,
* outlier builds,
* vampirism,
* absorption,
* mutation,
* passives that evolve,
* weird styles,
* anti-mage builds,
* monster-hunter vs duelist matchup logic

That is exactly what makes the system interesting.
It is also exactly what makes it hard.

Why others stopped earlier:

* A small stat system is easy.
* A freeform stat system with nonlinear growth, specialization, matchup logic, and outlier exceptions is much harder to keep coherent.

Complication for us:

* We can’t just build “level + skills.”
* We need rules for when the system says:
    * this is normal progression,
    * this is an outlier,
    * this is a temporary buff,
    * this is a permanent body change,
    * this exceeds normal scaling.

If we do not define those boundaries, the system will become incoherent long before level 300, let alone 1600.

⸻

5. NPC generation becomes expensive fast

It sounds easy to say:

* “generate believable stats and builds for all meaningful characters”

The actual problem is deciding:

* who counts as meaningful,
* when to generate them,
* when to persist them,
* when to revise them,
* and how much detail is worth storing.

Inner Self solved this by focusing on important NPC minds, not every person. Auto-Cards likewise emphasizes selective card generation and summaries rather than universal exhaustive simulation.  

Why others stopped:

* Generating stable profiles for every named actor quickly becomes too heavy and too noisy.
* If you persist everyone, the world bloats.
* If you persist almost no one, titles stop meaning anything.

Complication for us:

* We need strong thresholds:
    * background extra
    * locally relevant actor
    * persistent actor
    * major actor

Without that, performance and coherence both degrade.

⸻

6. Time coherence is deceptively hard

Most scripts do not really solve time. They gesture at it.

Why:

* The AI naturally uses vague phrases like “later,” “soon,” “that morning,” “tomorrow.”
* Travel, sleep, quest schedules, combat duration, and event recall all interact.
* AI Dungeon gives you hooks, but not a built-in world clock. You have to make one in state.  

Why people stop short:

* Time coherence requires persistent clock state, event timestamps, route memory, and contradiction detection.
* That is a lot of logic for something players only notice when it breaks.

Complication for us:

* If we do not make time a first-class system, everything else gets quietly undermined.

⸻

7. Inventory is harder than “add item name to a list”

A believable inventory system has to answer:

* Did the player really acquire it?
* Was it held, worn, packed, stored, or dropped?
* Is it stackable?
* Is it currency?
* Did it change condition?
* Was it consumed?
* Is it still visibly equipped?

Why others stopped:

* A noun list is cheap.
* A real possession model is much more expensive.

Complication for us:

* Inventory is one of the biggest false-finish points. A lot of systems look done because they can list items, but they still cannot maintain coherent equipment, condition, or story-bestowed changes.

⸻

8. “Meaning preservation” is much harder than “name preservation”

This is the ability problem you cared about most.

Auto-generated or emergent systems are naturally tempted to reduce things to:

* the ability name,
* a category guess,
* and one generic effect

That is easier to summarize and easier to trigger later.

Why others stopped there:

* It is much simpler than preserving:
    * origin context,
    * intended function,
    * observed use,
    * constraints,
    * discovered applications,
    * and current mastery stage

Complication for us:

* If we do not explicitly store why an ability exists and how it has been used, the engine will drift toward generic fantasy interpretations.

⸻

9. Immediate world mutation and long-term world memory are different problems

This is another hidden trap.

A system can be good at:

* generating buildings,
* creating location cards,
* or expanding lore

without being good at:

* preserving a broken wall,
* keeping a flooded cellar flooded,
* remembering that the owner lost an eye,
* or keeping soot damage across revisits

Auto-Cards was aimed at object permanence and plot-relevant card automation, which is useful, but that goal is not the same as a full mutable world-state simulation.  

Why others stopped:

* Worldbuilding and world mutation feel related, but they are not solved by the same logic.

Complication for us:

* We need immutable identity + mutable state everywhere it matters.

⸻

10. The AI is not a deterministic game engine

Even with perfect scripting, the model still narrates in a probabilistic way.

Why this matters:

* It may phrase the same kind of event very differently.
* It may understate or overstate outcomes.
* It may narrate around missing context creatively rather than correctly.
* It may imply consequences without making them explicit.

Why earlier systems stopped at “pretty good”:

* Once you go freeform, you are always mediating between structured state and fuzzy narration.

Complication for us:

* We can never fully “solve” this with parsing alone.
* We need:
    * compact truth packets,
    * consequence validation,
    * tentative states,
    * and failure-safe behavior.

⸻

11. Compatibility goals force simplification

Both Inner Self and Auto-Cards explicitly pitch themselves as broadly compatible/general-purpose systems. Inner Self emphasizes universal compatibility and zero immersion breaks; Auto-Cards emphasizes plug-and-play use, general-purpose usefulness, and suitability for both free and premium users.  

That matters because a script built for:

* many genres,
* many playstyles,
* free and premium contexts,
* and easy installation

will usually avoid:

* very strict simulation assumptions,
* very heavy world models,
* or very aggressive validation systems.

Why others stopped:

* Broad compatibility and deep simulation are often in tension.

Complication for us:

* Every time we make the engine more exact, we risk making it less universally plug-and-play.

⸻

12. The final 20% is where almost all the work is

This is the real answer to “why nobody finished this.”

A script can get:

* 60% of the feel
* with 20% of the work

You can make:

* auto stats,
* auto cards,
* some memory,
* some inventory,
* some skills

and it already feels impressive.

But the last 20% you want requires solving:

* edge cases,
* body changes,
* long-term persistence,
* card sync thresholds,
* outlier builds,
* contradictory narration,
* impossible fights,
* delayed trigger timing,
* and small-context degradation

That is where most projects stall.

⸻

The practical issue list we should watch for

These are the specific issues likely to hit us.

High-risk issues

* Over-triggering world mutations from ambiguous prose
* Genericizing new abilities because origin/use context is not saved strongly enough
* Inventory drift between equipped/held/stored/consumed states
* NPC bloat from persisting too many people
* Time contradictions accumulating silently
* Card sync over-updating and wasting context/runtime
* Outlier builds breaking level expectations
* Context packets growing too large and crowding out story quality
* Retried outputs double-applying state changes
* Immediate scene truth lagging behind because cards update too late

Medium-risk issues

* Reputation becoming too noisy to matter
* Quest logs drifting from actual plot
* Too many passive triggers causing accidental growth
* Build tags becoming vague sludge instead of useful classification
* Freeform parser confidence thresholds being too loose or too strict

Low-risk but annoying issues

* Item naming ugliness
* Too much debug clutter
* Unhelpful archetype summaries
* Cards becoming over-written prose blobs instead of useful structured mirrors

⸻

The actual safety-net rules we should adopt before building

Rule 1

No raw prose becomes canon without validation.

Rule 2

No major world mutation from one weak clue alone.

Rule 3

Player-state changes beat narrative convenience.

Rule 4

Current scene truth must come from state/context, not card timing alone.

Rule 5

Every subsystem must degrade gracefully under small context.

Rule 6

Important actors get persistent profiles; extras do not.

Rule 7

Abilities are defined by origin + use + observed effects, not title alone.

Rule 8

Outlier builds are allowed, but must be marked as exceptions, not silently treated as normal scaling.

Rule 9

Time advancement must always be explicit somewhere in the state.

Rule 10

If uncertain, fail soft instead of inventing certainty.

⸻

My honest verdict

The people before you probably did not fail because the idea was impossible.

They mostly stopped because:

* AI Dungeon’s scripting environment rewards compact, broadly compatible tricks over deep simulation,  
* Story Cards and memory timing make immediate persistence harder than it looks,  
* and the truly hard part is not building the first version, but building the version that survives hundreds of edge cases.

That means the project is possible, but only if we keep the build disciplined and treat these failure modes as first-class design constraints from the start.
